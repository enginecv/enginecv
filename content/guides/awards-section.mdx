Awards give a reader something rare on a CV: an outside opinion. Most of the document is you describing yourself, but an award says someone else looked at your work and rated it. Used well, a short list of awards raises the credibility of the whole page.

This guide goes through each field in the editor's awards form, shows how to explain an award so that an unfamiliar reader understands it, and explains how the section changes with your career stage. The people and awards in the examples are invented.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

A recruiter looks at an award for three things: who gave it, what it was for and how selective it was. The name alone rarely tells them, so the description does most of the work.

Applicant tracking systems read awards as plain text, so the usual advice about keywords holds. If an award relates to the role, such as a coding competition for an engineering job or a sales prize for a sales role, the words that describe the achievement help the match.

Readers also use awards to judge relevance. A prize in a field close to the job is a strong signal. A prize in an unrelated area still shows effort, but it should be short and sit lower down.

## Field by field

### Award name

Use the official title of the award, as the giving body would write it. If the title is generic, such as Employee of the Year, add the scope in the description so readers can tell whether it was a team, a region or a company-wide award.

### Issuer

Name the organisation, competition or institution that gave it. This is what lets a reader judge the weight of the award, so spell it out in full and avoid internal abbreviations.

### Date

Give the month and year, or just the year. If the award covers a period, such as a year of performance, use the year in which it was awarded.

### Description

This is where you earn the entry. In one or two sentences, say what the award recognised, how selective it was and what you did. If there were numbers, such as entrants, finalists or the size of the project, use them.

### Link

If the award has a public page, such as a results listing or an announcement, add it. A working link gives the claim a source a reader can check.

### Skills

Use the skills field to connect the award to the competencies it demonstrated. Choose the few that matter for the job you want, so the entry supports your other sections.

## Writing it well: before and after

The best award entries answer, in a line or two, what it was, who judged it and why you won.

**Before:** Won an innovation award at work in 2024.

**After:** Innovation Award, Harbourline Logistics, 2024. One of three winners out of 140 nominations for a route-planning tool that cut average delivery time by 12 percent.

The first version has no scale and no content. The second has the giver, the selectivity and the result, which is everything a reader needs.

A second example shows how to present a competition result.

**Before:** Took part in a national hackathon and did well.

**After:** First place, National Student Hackathon, 2023. Team of four, 90 teams entered. Built an accessibility checker for school websites in 36 hours and presented it to a jury of industry engineers.

The stronger entry gives the result, the field, the team and the scope, and it shows the work behind the prize.

A third example covers a scholarship, which is both an award and a form of recognition.

**Before:** Scholarship for good grades.

**After:** Dean's Merit Scholarship, Eastmoor University, 2021 to 2024. Awarded to the top five percent of the engineering cohort for three consecutive years, covering full tuition.

This version names the body, the criterion and the selectivity, so the reader understands why it matters.

## Variations by stage and situation

**Students and recent graduates.** Awards can balance a short work history. Include scholarships, competition results, academic prizes and recognition from clubs or societies, and say how many people competed. Keep school-level prizes to the best one or two.

**Early career.** Move from academic awards toward work-related recognition as soon as you have it. A team award or a manager's recognition, described with its context, is stronger than a university prize that is several years old.

**Experienced professionals.** Keep only awards tied to your field, such as industry honours, published recognition or notable client or company awards. If an award has been mentioned in your experience entry, avoid repeating it.

**Creative fields.** Awards are a standard part of the CV for designers, writers and filmmakers. Name the category and the number of entries, and link to the work or the announcement.

**Public sector and academia.** Grants, fellowships and research prizes belong here. Name the funding body, the amount if it is public, and what the money supported.

**Career changers.** Choose awards that show transferable strengths, such as leadership, customer results or analysis, and describe them in words that fit the new field.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is listing awards with no context. "Employee of the Month" tells a reader almost nothing without a sense of who chose it and how often it is given.

Another is padding. Certificates of participation and attendance are not awards, and listing them next to genuine recognition weakens both.

A third is exaggeration. If you were a finalist, say finalist, and if you shared an award with a team, say so. These claims are easy to check, and a small correction later costs more than modesty now.

A fourth is including awards that are too old to matter. If an award is more than a decade old and unrelated to your field, it takes space that recent work could use.

Finally, avoid long descriptions. One or two lines explain an award better than a paragraph, and the reader still has your experience to look at.

## Where awards belong on the page

Position matters as much as wording. If recognition is the strongest thing you have for a role, such as a competition win for a graduate, place the section high on the page, above or beside education. If your experience carries the case, keep awards lower down and let them confirm what the jobs already say.

Another option is to attach an award to the entry it belongs to. A prize won for work at a particular company can sit in that job's highlights, with the separate section kept for recognition that has no natural home. Choose one place for each award, because repeating it twice looks like padding.

Keep a private record too. Save the announcement, the certificate or the email that confirms the award, since some employers ask for proof when they make an offer, and a ready file saves a delay.

Finally, think about tone. State the award plainly and let the facts carry the weight. Words such as prestigious or highly competitive add little unless a number backs them up, whereas "one of three winners from 140 nominations" needs no adjective at all. The same restraint makes the rest of the CV more believable.

## A final check

For each award, ask whether a stranger can tell who gave it, what for, when and why it matters. If any of those answers is missing, add one line. Then ask whether it supports the role you are applying for, and remove it if not.

Order the section by relevance, with the most impressive and most related award first. A short, explained list persuades more than a long one that leaves the reader guessing.
