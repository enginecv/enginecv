References are the one part of a CV that other people complete. A good reference confirms your claims in the words of someone who watched you work, so choosing and preparing them carefully can matter as much as anything you write about yourself.

This guide covers each field in the editor's references form, how to choose and prepare your referees, and when to leave the section out altogether. The names and organisations used as examples are invented.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

Recruiters use references late in a hiring process, so the section is checked for completeness and for fit with your story. They ask whether the people hold roles that match the claims on your CV, and whether the contact details look current and usable.

Applicant tracking systems rarely score this section, so keywords matter little. What matters is clarity. A reader should see who the person is, how they know you and how to reach them without opening another document.

Many employers also read the section for judgment. Naming a former line manager and a senior colleague suggests you chose sensibly, while three friends with vague job titles raises questions.

## Field by field

### Full name

Use the name the person uses in their working life, with the title they would want if it is usual in your field. Check the spelling with them, because a misspelt name in a reference looks careless.

### Job title

Give their current title, or the title they held when you worked together, and be clear which it is. If it is the old title, add it to the description so the reader understands the connection.

### Organisation

Name the company or institution where you worked with them, not only where they are now. This is the link between the reference and your CV, so it should match an entry in your experience or education.

### Email

Use the address the person wants employers to use, usually a work address. Check that it is still active, and ask whether they prefer to be contacted by email first.

### Phone

Add a phone number only if the person has agreed to be called. Include the country code if the employer is abroad, and note the best time to call if it helps.

### Profile link

A professional profile link helps a recruiter confirm that the person exists and holds the role you say. Use it only with their consent, and prefer a work profile to a personal one.

### Note

The note is the place to explain the relationship in one line, for example that they managed you for two years or supervised your thesis. This helps a recruiter decide whom to call first, and it makes the reference easy to place.

## Writing it well: before and after

A reference entry should tell the reader who the person is and why they can speak for you. It should not read like a name in a list.

**Before:** Mr. Johnson, manager. Call me for details.

**After:** Daniel Johnson, Engineering Manager, Brightfield Systems. Line manager from 2022 to 2025, saw the full migration project from planning to launch. Prefers email first.

The first version leaves out the organisation and the relationship and adds no contact details. The second gives all of that, and it explains why this person is a good choice.

A second example improves a reference from outside a company.

**Before:** Client, happy with my work.

**After:** Priya Nair, Operations Director, Calder Logistics. Client on a six-month freelance project to rebuild the dispatch dashboard. Available for a call on weekday afternoons.

Naming the person, the project and the best time to call turns a vague claim into something a recruiter can act on.

A third example shows how to present an academic referee.

**Before:** Professor, university.

**After:** Prof. Elena Marković, Department of Computer Science, Riverside University. Supervised my final-year thesis on routing algorithms, 2024. Contact by email.

The better version names the person, the department and the work, which tells the reader exactly what she can speak to.

## Variations by stage and situation

**Students and recent graduates.** Use a thesis supervisor, a lecturer who knew your work well, or an internship manager. Employers understand that your list is short and academic.

**Experienced professionals.** Choose recent managers and senior colleagues who can speak to results. Include at least one person who saw you lead, and one who worked beside you.

**Career changers.** Pick referees who can speak to the skills that transfer, even if they come from a different field. A short note explaining the link helps the reader see why the choice makes sense.

**Applying abroad.** Check local custom. In some countries references are listed on the CV, in others they are provided only on request, and some employers expect signed letters instead.

**Confidential job searches.** If your current employer does not know you are looking, do not list your current manager. Say that references from current employment are available after an offer, and offer others from earlier roles.

**Freelancers and contractors.** Clients are strong referees. Name the project and the result, and make sure each client agrees to be contacted.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is listing someone without asking. A referee who is surprised by a call may say less than you hoped, or may not answer at all, and the employer notices.

The second is stale contact details. An email that bounces or a number that has changed ends the check, and it looks like poor preparation.

A third is choosing by seniority instead of knowledge. A director who barely knew you says less than a team lead who worked with you daily. Pick the person who can give specifics.

Another is filling space with the phrase "available on request". It uses a line and tells the reader what they already assume.

Finally, do not forget privacy. Contact details on a CV that is uploaded to many sites can be copied and misused, so consider sharing referee details only when an employer asks.

## Preparing the people you list

A good reference starts before the call. Tell each person which role you are applying for, send them the job description and a copy of your CV, and remind them of the projects you would like them to mention. Most people are glad to help and give better answers when they have the details fresh.

Thank them afterwards and tell them the outcome. Referees are a long-term relationship, and you will likely need them again.

Keep a list of three to five possible referees, and choose the best two or three for each application. That way the choice fits the role every time, and nobody is asked more often than they are happy with.

## Reference letters and recommendations

Some people hold written recommendations as well as, or instead of, a named contact. A letter signed by a former manager can be attached to an application when the employer accepts it, and a recommendation on a professional network page can be linked from your profile. Both are useful, but neither replaces a person who can answer follow-up questions.

If you hold a letter, keep it short and specific, dated and signed, and check that it still describes you accurately. Mention it in the note field of the matching reference, so a recruiter knows there is a document as well as a contact. Never edit a letter someone else wrote, and do not forward it more widely than the author would expect.

## A final check

Read the section as a recruiter about to make a call. Can you tell who each person is, how they know the candidate and the best way to reach them? If not, add the missing detail.

Then check consent and currency. Every person should have agreed, every email and number should work, and the list should be short. If the posting does not ask for references, consider whether the space is better used on your achievements.
