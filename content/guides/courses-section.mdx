A courses section is where you show that you keep learning and that the learning points the same way as the job. It matters most when your formal education does not cover a topic the role needs, when you are changing careers, or when a field moves faster than degrees do.

This guide covers each field in the editor's course form, how to choose what to list, how to describe a course without padding it, and how courses differ from certificates and education. The people, schools and platforms in the examples are invented.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

A recruiter scanning the courses section wants to see relevance and follow-through. Does the topic match the posting, and did the person finish? Titles that echo the vocabulary of the job make the connection obvious in a few seconds.

Applicant tracking systems read course titles, providers and any skills you attach, and some match them against the posting like any other text. That is a good reason to use the real title of the course, not a shortened or invented one, and to name the skills it covered in the skills field.

Readers also notice recency and proportion. A cluster of recent, related courses suggests direction. A long list of unrelated titles from ten years ago suggests a CV that has not been edited in a while.

## Field by field

### Course title

Use the full official title as the provider shows it, for example the name of the specialization or the programme, not just the topic. If the course is part of a series, the series name can go in the description.

### Institution

Name the school, company or platform that ran the course. Use the name a reader would recognise. If a university delivered the course through an online platform, the university is usually the stronger name to show.

### Link

Add the course page or your completion page when one exists. A link lets a reader check the content in seconds and see that you did the work. Leave it out if the page requires a login or will expire.

### Start and end dates

Give the period you took the course, or only the end date if you finished quickly. If the course is still running, mark it as current, and keep it only if you are a meaningful way through. Dates also show how recent your learning is, which is often the point.

### Location

Location is optional and mostly useful for in-person training, such as a bootcamp held in a particular city. For online courses it can be left blank, since it adds no information.

### Description

Use the description to say what the course covered and what you produced, in one to three short lines. Name the topics, the tools and any project you built. Avoid copying the provider's marketing text, because readers recognise it and it says nothing about you.

### Skills used

Add the skills the course gave you in practice, such as a language, a method or a tool. These can be pulled into your skills section later, so keep them specific and spelled the way the postings spell them.

## Writing it well: before and after

The best course entries say what you can now do, not just that you sat through the material.

**Before:** Data Science course, online. Learned lots of useful things about data.

**After:** Data Analysis with Python, Northbridge Online, 2025. Twelve-week programme on cleaning, joining and visualising datasets with pandas. Built a dashboard from public transport data as the final project.

The first version gives a topic and a feeling. The second gives the full title, the provider, the year, the content and a concrete result.

A second example turns a vague training line into evidence.

**Before:** Attended a leadership workshop.

**After:** Leading Small Teams, Harbor Training Centre, 2024. Two-day workshop on delegation, feedback and running one-to-ones. Applied it as team lead for a group of five on a spring release.

The honest rewrite names the provider and the length, then connects the training to something you did with it.

A third example handles a course you only partly finished.

**Before:** Machine learning, Coursera, did most of it.

**After:** Machine Learning Specialization, completed courses 1 and 2 of 3 (supervised learning, 2025). Implemented regression and classification models from scratch in notebooks.

State exactly what you completed. A precise partial result reads better than a vague claim of "most of it".

## Variations by stage and situation

**Students and recent graduates.** Courses can show initiative beyond the syllabus. Choose ones that extend your degree, and put them below education so the formal record leads.

**Career changers.** This section carries more weight for you. List the structured programmes that prepare you for the new field, attach projects where you can, and lead with the most relevant one.

**Experienced professionals.** Keep only recent courses that update your skills or fill a gap, such as a new tool or regulation. Older ones that your experience already proves can go.

**Technical roles.** Include the tools and versions in the description. Hiring managers in engineering and data often scan for them, and a project built during the course is strong evidence.

**Regulated or safety-related fields.** Mandatory training, such as first aid or workplace safety, often belongs in certificates if you hold a valid credential. Use courses for the learning that has no formal credential attached.

**Returning to work after a break.** Recent courses show that you kept up. Put the dates in clearly and add the practical work you did, so the gap looks like active time.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is listing everything. A page of course titles with no description reads like a transcript and hides the two or three that matter.

The second is vagueness. "Online marketing course" tells the reader nothing, while the full title, the provider and one outcome tell them everything.

A third is copying the provider's marketing description. It is easy to spot and gives no sense of what you did.

Another is mixing courses with credentials. If you passed an exam and hold a certificate, that belongs in the certificates section. Listing it twice wastes space and can look like padding.

Finally, do not list a course you could not discuss. Interviewers ask about content, and a title you cannot talk about hurts more than it helps.

## Choosing what to keep

Treat the section as a short, edited list. Put the posting beside your courses and mark each one that supports a requirement. Keep those, and ask of the rest whether it adds a skill or a signal that nothing else on the CV provides.

Order matters too. Put the most relevant and the most recent first, then fill in older ones only if they still matter. If you have more courses than the space allows, link your full learning record from your profile instead.

## A final check

Read each entry as a stranger would. Is the title complete, is the provider recognisable, is there a date, and does the description say what you built or learned? Fix whatever is missing.

Then check that every entry earns its place. Three well described courses that match the role beat ten titles. If you can say one honest sentence about what each course changed in your work, keep it.
