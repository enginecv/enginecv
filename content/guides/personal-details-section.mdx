The personal details section is the first thing a reader sees, and it has one job: make you easy to identify, place and contact. It sounds like a formality, but small choices in this block affect how the rest of the CV is read, how it is processed by software, and how much of your personal information you give away.

This guide covers each field in the editor's personal details form, the optional details that vary by country, and how to balance being reachable with protecting your privacy. The names and addresses used are invented.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

A recruiter looks at this block for three things within seconds: who you are, what you do and how to reach you. A clear name, a title that matches the role and a working email are enough to move on to the content.

Applicant tracking systems pull your name and contact details into their records. If those are formatted unusually, buried in an image or placed in a header the parser skips, the record can end up blank. Plain text in a normal order is safest.

Readers also use the block to judge care. A typo in an email, an unprofessional address or an out-of-date phone number can end an application before the content is read.

## Field by field

### Full name

Use the name you want to be called in the job and that matches your documents where it matters. If you go by a shorter or different name, use it, and make sure it is consistent across your CV, your profiles and your cover letter.

### Professional title

The title is a short line that places you, such as Frontend Developer or Registered Nurse. Match it to the role you want, not to a past job title that no longer describes you. Keep it plain, because a clever title is harder for people and software to match.

### Photo

A photo is optional, and whether to use one depends on the market. If you add one, choose a recent, well-lit head-and-shoulders image with a neutral background and use the crop tool to centre it. Remove it for applications where photos are discouraged.

### Email

Use one professional address, and use the label field to say which it is if you list more than one, for example work or personal. Check it for typos, and make sure you read that inbox often.

### Phone

Add a number you answer, written in international format with the country code if you apply abroad. Make sure your voicemail greeting is professional, since recruiters sometimes call without warning.

### Location

A city and country is enough for most applications. It tells the employer about time zones, commuting and right to work without publishing your street. Give more only when the employer asks for it.

### Links and profiles

Add the profiles that support your application, such as a professional network page, a portfolio or a code repository. Each link should work, be current and show work you are proud of. Remove any that are empty or out of date.

### Optional personal details

The form also offers details such as date of birth, nationality, driving licence, marital status, gender or pronouns, and similar items. These are optional, and the right choice depends on country and role, as described below.

## Writing it well: before and after

The best header is short, consistent and easy to act on.

**Before:** SuperCoder99@oldmail.com, I live at 14 Elm Street, Apartment 3B, Springfield, 555 01 2345 (call after 6)

**After:** Maya Lindqvist, Backend Developer. maya.lindqvist@example.com, +46 70 123 45 67, Gothenburg, Sweden. GitHub and portfolio links.

The first version uses an unprofessional address, gives away a full street address and mixes instructions into the data. The second gives a name, a title and clean contact details.

A second example shows how to handle a title.

**Before:** Ninja Rockstar Developer and Problem Solver

**After:** Full-Stack Developer, TypeScript and React

A plain title with two key skills is clear to a reader and easy for software to match to a posting.

A third example improves a links line.

**Before:** My website: coolsite.example, LinkedIn: see my page, GitHub: github.example/old-account-2019

**After:** Portfolio: maya-lindqvist.example. Profile: linkedin.example/in/maya-lindqvist. Code: github.example/maya-lindqvist.

The better version uses working, current links with clear labels, so every click leads to something relevant.

## Variations by country and situation

**Continental Europe.** A photo and some personal details may be normal, and the header is often more detailed. Check the local convention and whether the employer expects it.

**United States, United Kingdom and Canada.** Leave out the photo and sensitive details such as age, marital status and nationality. Employers there often prefer not to receive them, to avoid bias.

**Applying abroad.** Use international phone format, name the country in your location, and mention your right to work if it is not obvious. This saves a round of questions.

**Roles that need a licence or clearance.** Add the relevant item, such as a driving licence, when the posting requires it. Use the optional details for facts the job asks about and skip the rest.

**Privacy-conscious applicants.** Share the minimum. A professional email, a city and a link to a portfolio are enough, and you can provide more once a process is under way.

**Career changers.** Choose a title that reflects the direction you are heading, and support it with links that show relevant work.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is an unprofessional email address. It is the first thing a recruiter reads, and it sets a tone you may not want.

The second is sharing too much. A full street address, an identity number or a long list of private details can be misused when a CV is uploaded to many sites.

A third is leaving out the title. Without it, a reader has to infer your field from the content, and software has less to match against.

Another is broken or outdated links. A portfolio that does not load or a profile with no content suggests carelessness.

Finally, do not rely on an image for your contact details. Text inside a picture is invisible to parsers and cannot be copied, so make sure your details are in plain text.

## Privacy and safety

A CV travels. It may be stored on job boards, shared with agencies and forwarded by email, so assume that anything on it can be copied. Keep sensitive information such as identity numbers, exact home address and family details off the page unless a specific, trusted process asks for them.

Use an email address dedicated to your job search if you can, so spam and scams stay out of your main inbox. Be careful with unsolicited messages after you post a CV online, and never send identity documents before you have confirmed an offer is real.

If you share your CV as a file, check that it does not contain hidden information. Clear the document properties and any comments before you send it.

## A final check

Read the header as a recruiter would. Do you know in five seconds who this is, what they do and how to reach them? If anything is unclear, rewrite it.

Then test every detail. Send an email to the address, call the number, click each link and read the location line. Remove optional details that are not needed in the market you are applying to, and keep only what helps the reader contact you.
