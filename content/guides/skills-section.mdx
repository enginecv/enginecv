The skills section has two readers who want opposite things. The software that scans your CV wants exact, standard keywords it can match against a job description. The human who reads it afterwards wants a short, believable picture of what you can do. A good skills section serves both, and a bad one serves neither.

This guide covers what to include, how to group and order it, how to show proficiency and the mistakes that turn a skills list into noise. The skills and names in the examples are invented, so replace them with your own.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

An applicant tracking system compares the words in your CV with the words in the posting. If the job asks for PostgreSQL and your skills list says only databases, the match is weaker than it needs to be. Use the exact term that employers use, in its common spelling, and include the long form once if an abbreviation is not universal.

Recruiters read the section differently. They look for a quick sign that you cover the main requirements, then check whether the rest of the CV backs it up. A skill that shows up in your skills list and again in a job bullet is far more convincing than a skill that appears only once, in a list.

Both readers punish padding. A list of thirty tools, half of which you touched once, makes the real strengths harder to see.

## Field by field

### Skill name

Use one specific, standard name per skill, for example Python, Project scheduling or Wound care. Avoid joining several skills in one item with slashes, since parsers and readers handle separate items better.

### Group

Skills can be arranged in groups with names such as Languages, Frameworks, Tools or Clinical. Groups help a reader find what matters in a long list. Keep the number of groups small, usually three to five, and give them plain names.

### Level

A level, from beginner to expert, tells the reader how deep your knowledge goes. Use it where depth is meaningful and honest. If every skill is marked as expert, the marker has no value. A few skills at a high level and the rest unmarked is more credible.

### Keywords

The keywords field lets you attach related terms to one skill, such as the libraries you use with a language. This is useful for ATS matching without cluttering the main list. Keep to terms you could discuss in an interview.

### Description

An optional description can add a short context, such as years of use or the kind of work. Use it sparingly. One line that gives scale or context does more than a general sentence.

### Icon and display

Where icons are available, they can make a technical list quicker to scan. Choose whether to show the name, the icon or both, and keep that choice the same across the list so the section looks tidy. Icons are decoration, not evidence, so make sure the names carry the meaning.

## Choosing which skills to list

Start from the job, not from yourself. Read the posting and mark the skills it names, then check each against your own experience. Include the ones you can back with an example. Add a few more that are standard for the field even if the posting does not name them, since they signal that you know the area.

Next, separate hard skills from soft ones. Hard skills are specific and testable, such as a programming language, a clinical procedure, or a software package. Soft skills such as communication are better shown through your experience bullets than listed as words.

Finally, drop what is outdated or too basic. Listing a word processor in a skills section, unless the job is specifically about document work, tells the reader nothing, and listing a tool you last used a decade ago invites doubt.

## Writing it well: before and after

Compare how a skills section looks before and after it is focused on a target role.

**Before:** Computers, internet, Microsoft Office, hard-working, good communication, team player, programming, databases, quick learner.

**After:** Languages: Python, SQL, TypeScript. Data tools: PostgreSQL, dbt, Airflow. Practices: code review, test-driven development, data modelling.

The first version is a mix of obvious items and personality claims. The second names specific skills, groups them in a way a reader can scan, and mirrors the language of a data engineering posting.

Here is an example from a non-technical field, with a fictional candidate.

**Before:** Patient care, medical knowledge, teamwork, computer skills.

**After:** Clinical: wound care, medication administration, IV therapy, patient assessment. Systems: electronic patient records, early-warning score charts. Languages: English (fluent), Spanish (conversational).

The after version replaces broad claims with the actual procedures and systems used on a ward, and adds languages that matter in the setting.

One more example shows how levels can help without taking over.

**Before:** JavaScript (expert), HTML (expert), CSS (expert), React (expert), Node.js (expert), Git (expert), Docker (expert).

**After:** JavaScript and TypeScript (expert), React (proficient), Node.js (proficient), Docker (competent), Git.

The first list marks everything as expert, which no reader believes. The second shows a realistic spread and leaves a basic tool unmarked.

## Variations by role and career stage

**Early career.** Skills carry more weight when you have little experience. Include technical skills from study and projects, and mention tools you used in internships. Back each with a project or module so it does not float free.

**Mid career.** Shift the list toward the skills used in your current and recent roles. Remove what you no longer use, and make sure the top items match the next role you want.

**Senior and leadership.** Hard skills matter less than scope. Keep a short list of core technical or domain skills, and add leadership skills in concrete terms, such as budgeting, hiring, vendor management or regulatory compliance, rather than general qualities.

**Technical roles.** Group by type, such as languages, frameworks, infrastructure and tools. Order within each group by strength and relevance, and use keywords for related libraries.

**Non-technical roles.** Use plain, field-specific terms, such as procedures, software, standards or methods. Mention the systems you operate, since they are often what the posting names.

**Career changers.** Lead with transferable skills that match the target role, and put the new skills you are building next to them. Link them to a course, certificate or project in other sections so they are not just claims.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is writing a list with no focus. A reader who sees thirty items cannot tell what you are best at, and the real strengths are lost.

Another is relying on adjectives. Hard-working, motivated and passionate are not skills, and they use up space that could carry a keyword.

Rating bars and percentage dials cause trouble too. A bar showing 85 percent means nothing, because nobody knows what the other 15 percent is. Named levels are clearer and easier to defend.

A fourth mistake is claiming skills you cannot demonstrate. Interviews often start from the skills list, so every item is a possible question.

The last mistake is leaving the list unchanged for every application. Reorder it for each job, and cut what does not apply. A skills section that mirrors the posting is the one that gets read.

## A final check

Look at the top five skills in your list and ask whether they are the ones this employer cares about most. Check that each can be backed up somewhere else in the CV. Remove anything you would hesitate to discuss, and remove any item that would be true of almost every applicant.

When the skills section is short, specific and honest, it supports your experience instead of repeating it.
