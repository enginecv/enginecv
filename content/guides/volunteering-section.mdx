Volunteering and memberships show what you do when nobody is paying you. For many applicants, especially students and career changers, they are the clearest evidence of initiative, teamwork and responsibility, and they often supply skills that a short work history cannot.

This guide covers each field in the editor's organisations form, how to describe a role so it counts as experience, and how to decide what to include. The people and organisations in the examples are invented.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

A recruiter reads this section for evidence that matches the job: leadership, reliability, community involvement or a skill the posting names. They look for a role, a period and a result, the same as in a work entry.

Applicant tracking systems read the organisation name, the role and the description. Use clear titles such as treasurer, coordinator or mentor, and name the tools and skills you used, so a search for those terms can find you.

Readers also look for commitment and consistency. A role held for two years suggests dependability, while many short, unrelated entries look scattered. Quality and fit beat quantity here.

## Field by field

### Organisation name

Use the official name of the organisation, club, charity or association. Pick the form people would recognise. If it is a local branch of a larger body, you can add the branch in the description.

### Role

State what you did, not only that you belonged. Titles such as volunteer coordinator, committee member, mentor or event lead say far more than member. If your role changed, show the most senior one and mention the earlier one in the description.

### Link

Add the organisation's website or your profile page there when it helps a reader confirm the role. Skip it if the page is out of date or reveals more about the organisation than you want to connect to your application.

### Start and end dates

Give the period you took part. For ongoing roles, mark them as current. The length matters, because a long commitment reads as dependable, so do not hide it by leaving out the dates.

### Location

Location is optional. Add it for in-person work, such as a local charity or a regional chapter. For remote or online communities it can be left blank.

### Description

The description is where this section gets its value. Say what you were responsible for, what you did and what changed. Use one to three short lines, lead with an action and add a number where you honestly have one.

### Skills used

List the skills you used or built, such as event planning, budgeting, public speaking, a programming language or a tool. These can feed your skills section, and they connect the volunteering to the requirements of the job.

## Writing it well: before and after

A strong entry reads like a job entry: a clear role, a scope and an outcome.

**Before:** Helped at a local animal shelter on weekends.

**After:** Volunteer Coordinator, Greenfield Animal Shelter, 2023 to 2025. Scheduled 25 weekend volunteers across feeding, cleaning and adoption days. Introduced a shared rota that cut missed shifts from about one a week to almost none.

The first version says you were present. The second gives a role, a scale and a measurable improvement.

A second example turns a membership into evidence of contribution.

**Before:** Member of a coding club.

**After:** Mentor, Riverside Coding Club, 2024 to present. Run a weekly beginner session for around 12 students in Python. Wrote a four-week starter curriculum that the club now reuses each term.

Naming the role, the audience and the lasting result shows contribution, not just attendance.

A third example shows how to present committee work.

**Before:** Student society committee.

**After:** Treasurer, University Debate Society, 2022 to 2023. Managed a yearly budget of 3,000 euros, tracked spending and secured two sponsorships that covered a national tournament trip.

Money handled, a decision made and a result delivered are exactly the proof an employer looks for.

## Variations by stage and situation

**Students and recent graduates.** This section can carry real weight. Lead with the roles where you held responsibility, such as society committees, tutoring or event teams, and describe them like jobs.

**Career changers.** Choose activities that practise the skills of the new field, such as a data project for a charity or marketing for a local group. They give you evidence from the field you are entering.

**Experienced professionals.** Keep it short and relevant. Board roles, mentoring and professional bodies show standing, and they are best placed after your experience.

**Technical roles.** Open-source contributions, community moderation and meetup organising all count. Link to the project or profile and name the technologies.

**Returning to work after a break.** Volunteering shows you stayed active and kept skills current. Put dates in clearly and describe the responsibilities, so the period reads as purposeful.

**Applying abroad.** Volunteering is valued differently by country. Employers in some places expect to see it, in others it is a minor note, so check the norms for the market you are applying to.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is a list of names with no roles or results. "Red Cross" or "Rotary" alone tells the reader nothing about what you did.

The second is listing too much. Ten small activities hide the two that matter, so choose the ones that fit the role and drop the rest.

A third is overstating a role. Calling yourself a manager for a small group of volunteers invites questions in an interview, and it is better to describe what you actually led.

Another is ignoring the audience. An activity that is meaningful to you can still distract a reader, so ask whether it supports the application.

Finally, do not leave out the dates. Without them the reader cannot judge the commitment, and the entry loses much of its value.

## Turning activity into evidence

Look at each activity and ask three questions. What was I responsible for? What did I do that someone could check? What changed because of it? Even a short answer to each gives you the material for a good description.

Numbers help when they are real, such as the people you managed, the money you handled or the hours you gave. If you have no numbers, describe the scope in words, for example the size of the event or the length of the programme.

Ask the organisation, too. Someone who ran the group often remembers results you have forgotten, and they may be willing to act as a reference.

## Memberships versus active roles

The organisations form covers two different things, and it helps to treat them differently. A membership says you belong to a professional body, a union, a standards group or a community. It carries weight when the body is respected in your field and when you took part, for example by attending events, serving on a working group or contributing to its publications.

An active role says you did work. Treasurer, coordinator, mentor and event lead all describe responsibility, so they deserve a full description. If you hold both a membership and a role in the same body, list the role, since it already implies the membership.

For a passive membership, keep the entry short: the name, the role of member and the start date. Spend your words on the roles where you made something happen.

## A final check

Read the section as if it were a second experience section. Does each entry have a role, a period and a result, and does it connect to something the job asks for? Rewrite or remove whatever does not.

Then check proportion. A short list of strong entries beats a long one. If you can explain each item in a sentence in an interview, it belongs on the CV.
