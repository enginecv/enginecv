A publications section tells a reader that your work has been judged fit to be shared. In research, higher education, medicine and engineering it is often a requirement, and in industry a few well-chosen articles or talks can set you apart from people with similar jobs.

The challenge is the same everywhere: show the strongest items, make them easy to find and describe your own part clearly. This guide walks through each field in the editor's publications form, shows how to describe a piece of work, and explains how the section differs for researchers, practitioners and early-career applicants. The authors, titles and venues in the examples are invented.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

Hiring panels in academic and research roles read a publication list closely. They look at the venue, the year, your role and how the work relates to the post. They are also quick to notice padding, such as a conference poster listed as a paper.

For non-academic roles, a reader scans for relevance. A talk at an industry meetup or an article on a recognised engineering blog can show communication skills and expertise that a job title cannot. Applicant tracking systems match on the subject words in the title and description, so use the terms of your field.

Readers also look for evidence that the work exists. A DOI, a link to a publisher page or a repository entry turns a citation into something a reader can open, and it protects you from the doubts that unsupported claims raise.

## Field by field

### Title

Use the exact title, in the case the publisher uses. For a book chapter, give the chapter title, and name the book in the publisher field or the description. If the title is very long, keep it whole, because truncated titles are hard to search for.

### Publisher or venue

Name the journal, conference, publisher or outlet in its recognised form. A reader judges the weight of the work largely from this, so avoid abbreviations that outsiders would not know, or spell them out the first time.

### Date

Give the year, and the month if it helps, such as for a conference. Use the publication date, not the date you submitted the work. For forthcoming items, use the status in the description and not a made-up date.

### Description

Use the description for your role, a one-line summary of the contribution and, if you have it, a sign of impact. A line such as "first author; proposes a lighter method for sensor calibration; cited 40 times" gives a reader what a bare citation cannot.

### Link

Add a DOI, a publisher page, a preprint or a repository entry. Prefer permanent links to temporary ones. If the work is behind a paywall, link to a free preprint or an author version where the publisher allows it.

### Skills

Attach the methods and technologies the work used. This helps connect a research item to a practical requirement in a posting, which matters most when you are moving from research to industry.

## Writing it well: before and after

Strong entries are complete, consistent and honest about your part.

**Before:** Paper about machine learning in healthcare, 2023.

**After:** Doe, J., Rivera, M., and Chen, L. (2023). Early detection of sepsis with lightweight gradient-boosted models. Journal of Clinical Data Science, 12(4), 233 to 245. First author; designed the feature set and ran the evaluation on 18,000 patient records.

The first entry has no title, venue or role. The second can be found, checked and understood, and it shows a specific contribution.

A second example shows how to present a talk, which is a common item outside academia.

**Before:** Gave some talks at tech events.

**After:** Keeping Build Times Under Five Minutes. Talk at Northfield Developer Days, 2024, 300 attendees. Describes how our team cut a monorepo build from 24 to 4 minutes; slides and recording linked.

The stronger version names the talk, the event, the audience and the content, and a reader can watch it.

A third example handles work in progress, which is easy to overstate.

**Before:** Paper on language models, forthcoming.

**After:** Under review: A benchmark for low-resource sentence alignment. Submitted to the International Conference on Language Resources, March 2025. Preprint available.

This version says plainly that the paper is under review, names the venue and offers a preprint, so nobody has to guess its status.

## Variations by stage and situation

**Researchers and academics.** Use the citation style of your field and order the list by type, such as journal articles first, then conference papers, then other items, with newest first inside each group. Mark your role clearly, and keep submitted work in a separate group.

**Graduate students and early-career researchers.** Include theses, posters, workshop papers and preprints, and label each by type so the reader sees what stage it reached. Add the supervisor or group when it helps, and avoid inflating a poster into a paper.

**Industry practitioners.** Select two to five items related to the job: articles on a company engineering blog, conference talks, white papers, patents, open standards contributions. Say what you wrote or presented and what the audience did with it.

**Writers and journalists.** Choose representative pieces by publication, subject and date, and link to them. A short list of strong, relevant bylines works better than a long archive.

**People moving from academia to industry.** Group your publications by theme and describe the practical skills behind them, such as data analysis, experiment design or technical writing. Keep the full list elsewhere and link to it.

**Applying internationally.** Check whether the employer expects a full list, a selected list or a link to a profile on a recognised research database. Adjust the length to match, and keep the formatting consistent.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is padding. Listing every poster, abstract and internal report next to peer-reviewed work blurs the picture, and experienced readers notice it immediately.

Another is hiding your role. In a co-authored paper, the reader needs to know whether you led the work or contributed a part. A short phrase answers the question and avoids awkward interview moments.

A third is inconsistent formatting. If one entry uses initials and another full names, or if dates appear in different places, the list looks careless. Choose one style and apply it to every entry.

A fourth is claiming status you do not have. Items that are submitted or under review are not published, and listing them as published or forthcoming is easy to check and damaging.

Finally, dead links undo the effort. Test every link before you send the CV, and prefer persistent identifiers.

## Keeping the list maintainable

A publication list is a record you will extend for years, so set it up to be easy to update. Keep one master version with every item, then cut a short version for each application. The master holds the full citation, the identifier and your role, and the short version holds only what the reader needs.

Check the details when you add an entry, not months later. Author names, page numbers and years are easy to get wrong from memory, and a wrong DOI is worse than none. If a publisher changes a link, update it in the master straight away.

It also helps to keep a public profile on a recognised research database or your own site, and link to it from the CV. That gives a reader who wants more the full list, while your CV stays focused on the items that matter for the role.

## A final check

For each entry, check that a reader could find the work, see your role and understand why it is on the page. If any of those is missing, add a link, a role or a line of description. Then ask whether the item helps with this particular application.

Keep the list in a clear order, group it by type if it is long, and trim it to what the reader needs. A concise, verifiable list reads as confident, and it keeps attention on your strongest work.
