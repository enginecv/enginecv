The professional summary is the first thing under your name, so it is read before almost anything else. Its job is narrow: tell a busy reader who you are, what you have done that matters, and what kind of role you are after, in a few lines. Everything below it is evidence for that opening claim.

This guide covers the summary box in the editor's personal details, a structure that works for most people, and how the summary changes depending on your situation. The example names and companies are invented.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

A recruiter scanning a pile of CVs spends a few seconds on the top third of the page. If the summary gives a clear job title, a rough level of experience and one or two concrete strengths, they know whether to keep reading. If it gives a list of personality traits, they skim past it.

An applicant tracking system treats the summary as ordinary text. It matters for matching because it is a natural place to use the role's own vocabulary: the job title, the main tools and the domain. If a posting asks for a "site reliability engineer" and your summary says "systems person who keeps things running", a keyword search may not connect the two. Use the plain title a hiring manager would type.

The summary is also where a reader decides whether the rest of the CV is worth the effort. A confident, specific opening makes them read your jobs more generously. A vague one makes them look for reasons to stop.

## What the summary box holds

In the editor the summary is a single text box. There are no sub-fields, so the structure comes from you. A reliable pattern has four parts, usually in this order.

### Who you are

Open with your professional identity: a job title or a short role description, plus a level or length of experience. For example, "Backend developer with seven years in payments" or "Recent nursing graduate with 600 hours of clinical placements". This single phrase does most of the work, so make it specific.

### Your strongest proof

Add one or two facts that show the claim is true. Numbers help when you have them, such as the size of a system, a team, a budget or an improvement. If you do not have numbers, name the most relevant responsibility or the most recognisable employer or domain.

### Your angle

If something sets you apart for this kind of role, say it. It could be a specialism, a rare combination of skills, or the setting you work in best, such as early-stage teams or regulated industries. Keep it factual, not a self-description.

### What you want

End with the direction you are heading, matched to the role you are applying for. This is the part you change most often. One short clause is enough, such as "looking for a platform engineering role in a product company".

## Writing it well: before and after

The weak versions below share a problem: they could describe thousands of people. The stronger versions could only describe the person who wrote them.

**Before:** Hard-working and motivated professional with excellent communication skills, looking for a challenging position in a dynamic company.

**After:** Customer support lead with five years in SaaS, managing a team of eight and cutting average first-response time from six hours to ninety minutes. Looking for a head of support role in a growing product company.

The first version lists adjectives that every applicant uses. The second gives a title, a setting, a scale and a result, then states the next step.

Here is a graduate example, where experience is thin and the evidence has to come from somewhere else.

**Before:** Recent graduate who is passionate about technology and eager to learn.

**After:** Computer science graduate from Eastmoor University with a final-year project on a bus-arrival prediction app used by 300 students. Comfortable with Python, SQL and Git, and looking for a junior data engineering role.

The stronger version uses the degree, a real project with a user count, the tools and the target role. It does not pretend to have work experience, and it does not need to.

One more example, for a person moving into a new field.

**Before:** Experienced teacher seeking a new career in software.

**After:** Secondary school maths teacher of nine years who completed a web development bootcamp and shipped a timetable tool now used by 40 staff at my school. Looking for a junior front-end role where classroom clarity and patience count for something.

This version turns the change of direction into a story with evidence. The old career is an asset, the new skills are proven by something built, and the reader can see why the move makes sense.

## Variations by stage and situation

**Students and recent graduates.** Lead with your degree and the most relevant project, placement or internship. Name two or three tools. Avoid claiming years of experience you do not have, and avoid the word "passionate", which reads as filler.

**Early career.** Replace the degree with your first job title, and put one measurable result up front. A line about the kind of team you want to join is fine.

**Mid-career specialists.** Name your specialism precisely and tie it to a scale or an outcome. "Frontend engineer focused on performance and accessibility" is better than "full-stack developer with broad skills", even if you can do both.

**Senior and leadership roles.** Lead with scope: people, budget, systems, markets. Describe the decisions you owned and the results that followed. A senior summary should also hint at how you work, but through a fact, such as "built and mentored a team of twelve", not an adjective.

**Career changers.** Name the target role, explain the bridge in one sentence and point to evidence you have already produced. The summary is where you control how your history is read, so use it.

**Returning after a break.** State your professional identity first, then mention recent training or projects that show you are current. A career break does not need an apology, and one neutral line is enough if you choose to mention it.

**Technical and creative fields.** Add a link to your portfolio or repository in the contact details, not in the summary, and let the summary name the kind of work you do and your strongest tools or media.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is a summary made entirely of adjectives. "Dynamic, motivated and detail-oriented" cannot be checked, so it is ignored. Replace each adjective with a fact that would make a reader reach the same conclusion on their own.

The second is length. A summary of ten lines is a second cover letter, and it pushes your real evidence below the fold. If it needs more than four sentences, some of it belongs in the work experience section.

The third is repeating your job history in miniature. The summary should not list every employer in order. Use it to pull out the thread that connects them.

A fourth is being generic about the target. If the same summary could be sent to any employer, it is not doing its job. Adjust the last sentence and one keyword for each role.

Finally, watch for claims that your CV cannot back up. If the summary says "expert in cloud architecture", a reader will look for a job that proves it. Make sure the evidence is on the page.

## A final check

Read the summary aloud and ask three questions. Does it name a role or a field in plain words? Does it contain at least one fact a stranger could not guess? Would it still be true, and useful, if you sent it to a different company in the same field with the last sentence changed?

If the answer to any of them is no, rewrite that part. When the summary is right, the rest of the CV only has to prove it.
