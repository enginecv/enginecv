The work experience section is the part of a CV that most readers open first and judge hardest. A recruiter with a stack of applications spends seconds on each, so this section has to answer one question quickly: has this person already done something like the job we are hiring for?

This guide covers each field in the work experience form in the same order the editor shows them, then walks through rewrites, role variations and the mistakes that show up most often. The names and employers in the examples are invented, so replace the details with your own.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

Two readers see your work experience. The first is software: an applicant tracking system that parses your CV into fields such as job title, employer, dates and description. If those are missing or hidden inside an odd layout, the parser files you wrongly and a person may never read the document. The second is a human who skims for recent titles, the length of each stay, and a few concrete results.

Both readers reward the same habits. Put each role in a consistent shape, newest first. Make the title and the employer easy to find. Keep dates complete. Then spend your words on evidence: scope, outcomes and the tools you used, not a list of duties anyone in the role would share.

Plain structure also helps you. When every entry follows the same pattern, a reader can compare your jobs at a glance and see growth from one role to the next.

## Field by field

### Job title

Use the title you would want a reference to confirm, and keep it recognisable. If your official title was an internal code such as "Associate II", add what the role really was, for example "Associate II (Support Engineer)". Recruiters search by familiar titles, and so do parsers.

Do not inflate. A title that does not match what your former manager says will cause trouble at the reference stage, and it is easy to check.

### Employer

Write the company name as people know it, not a legal entity string. When the employer is not well known, add a few words of context in the description, such as the industry or size, so the reader can place it. The optional link field is useful for a company site or a product page, but only add one that still works.

### Location

City and country is enough. If the role was remote, say so once and move on. Leave out street addresses; they add nothing and take space.

### Start and end dates

Give month and year for both, and use "Present" for a current job. Consistent dates help a parser calculate your experience, and they help a person trust the timeline. Avoid year-only dates for recent roles, since they look like an attempt to hide short stays.

### Description and bullets

This is where the section is won or lost, so it gets its own space below. In the editor, the description accepts light formatting, which means you can use a short line of context followed by bullets.

### Skills used

If your editor offers a field for the tools and skills used in a role, fill it in with the specific ones you used, not everything you have ever touched. It gives parsers keywords that sit next to real work, which is stronger than a list at the bottom of the page.

## Writing bullets that carry weight

A good bullet answers three things: what you did, how big it was, and what changed. You do not need all three in every line, but a section where none of the bullets has a number, a scale or an outcome reads as a job description, not a record of achievement.

Start with a strong verb in the correct tense: present tense for your current job, past tense for earlier ones. Name the thing you worked on, then add the result. When you do not have exact figures, use honest estimates and mark them as such, or describe the scale in other terms such as team size, number of customers or frequency.

**Before:** Responsible for customer support tickets and helping the team.

**After:** Resolved 40 to 60 customer tickets a week for a 12-person SaaS team, cutting the average first reply from six hours to under two by writing a shared answer library.

The first version tells the reader a duty. The second shows scale, a measurable change and the method, all in one line.

Here is a second pair, this time for an engineering role with a fictional candidate.

**Before:** Worked on the backend of the company's web application using various technologies.

**After:** Rebuilt the order-export service for the company's web shop in Go and PostgreSQL, taking exports of 20,000 orders from nine minutes to under forty seconds.

Notice what changed. The after version names the thing built, the tools, the size of the data and the improvement. A hiring manager can picture the work, and a parser finds the right keywords where they belong.

A third pair shows how to handle a role where results are hard to measure.

**Before:** Helped with events and general office tasks.

**After:** Organised six client events a year for up to 150 guests, managing venue contracts and a catering budget of about 25,000 euros without an overspend.

Even in roles without sales figures, you can usually state a count, a budget, a frequency or an audience.

## Variations by seniority and role

How you write the section should change as your career does.

**Early career.** You have fewer jobs, so each one gets more space. Lean on internships, projects and part-time work, and show learning: tools picked up, responsibility added, feedback acted on. Keep the section to what fits well on one page, with the strongest entries first.

**Mid career.** Show progression. If you were promoted, list the new title under the same employer as its own entry with its own dates, so the climb is visible. Give your latest role the most bullets and make them about outcomes, not tasks.

**Senior and leadership.** Scope matters more than activity. State team sizes, budgets, systems or markets you were accountable for, and the decisions you owned. Older roles shrink to a line or two, since the reader assumes you did the basics.

**Career changers.** Choose the experience that transfers and describe it in the language of the new field. A teacher moving into training might highlight designing materials and presenting to groups, while a hospitality manager moving into operations might highlight scheduling, budgets and supplier management. A short summary above the section helps the reader connect the dots.

**Freelancers and contractors.** Group short engagements under one entry with a clear label such as "Freelance web developer", and use bullets to show the types of client and the most relevant projects. Do not list twenty tiny contracts as twenty jobs.

## Common mistakes

Most weak work experience sections fail in predictable ways.

The first is listing duties instead of results. A bullet that could appear unchanged on anyone's CV for the same title is a duty. Replace it with something only you did.

The second is length. Cramming every task into a dense block hides the best material. Fewer, stronger bullets win.

The third is vague or unexplained jargon. Internal project names and acronyms mean nothing to a stranger. Say what the thing was in plain terms.

The fourth is inconsistent formatting, such as mixed date styles or bullets that switch between tenses. It signals carelessness and can confuse parsers.

The fifth is exaggeration. Claiming sole credit for team results or inventing figures puts you at risk the moment an interviewer asks a follow-up question. Be ready to explain every number you write.

The last is ignoring the job you are applying for. Your work experience is not a fixed document. Reorder bullets, trim the ones that do not help, and mirror the language of the posting where it honestly describes what you did.

## A quick check before you export

Read each entry and ask what a stranger would learn that they could not guess from the title alone. If the answer is nothing, rewrite the bullets. Then check the basics: dates complete and consistent, titles you can defend, no duplicated lines, and every bullet short enough to scan.

When you are happy with it, build the rest of your CV around it. A strong summary, a clean skills list and a matching education section turn good experience into a document a recruiter can read in half a minute.
