A projects section lets you show work that a job title cannot. It is the place for the app you built, the research you ran, the campaign you planned or the product you launched, whether it happened inside a job, a course or your own time.

For many applicants it is also the most persuasive part of the CV, because a project shows how you think and what you finish. This guide walks through the editor's projects form, shows how to describe a project in a way a reader can verify, and explains how the section changes with your experience. ## What recruiters and ATS look for

A hiring manager reading a project wants to answer four questions quickly. What was the problem? What did you personally do? How did you do it? What happened as a result? If an entry answers those in two or three lines, it works.

Applicant tracking systems read this section for keywords, so the tools, methods and domain terms in the entry matter. A project described as "a web application" gives a parser very little, while "a React and Node.js booking application with a PostgreSQL database" gives it names to match against the posting.

Recruiters also use projects to judge initiative. A project that someone actually used, even a few people, reads far better than a tutorial copied step by step. Where a project has real users, traffic, downloads or feedback, say so.

## Field by field

### Project title

Use the name the project is known by. If the name is cryptic, add a plain description after it, such as "Tidewatch, a tide-table app for sailors". Reviewers should not have to click a link to learn what it is.

### Subtitle

The subtitle is the place for the context in a few words: the kind of project and its setting. Examples are "Personal project", "Final-year thesis", "Volunteer work for a local charity" or "Team of four, hackathon winner". It tells the reader how much weight to give the entry.

### Description

State the problem and your approach in one or two sentences. Say what you built or did, not what the project "aims to" do. Avoid describing the tutorial or the framework. Describe the thing the user would see.

### Highlights

Use the highlights for results and decisions: what changed, what you measured, what you chose and why. Start each with a verb. If you have numbers, use them, such as users, response time, cost saved or test coverage. If you do not, describe an outcome a reader can picture.

### Keywords and skills

Add the technologies, tools and methods you used. Keep them accurate and specific, and include only those you could discuss in an interview. These also feed the way the section is matched against a posting.

### Dates

Give a start and an end date, or the years only. For a project that is ongoing, mark it as current. Dates matter because they show how recent your work is and how long you sustained it.

### Link and linked repositories

The link field is for the live project or a write-up, and the repository links are for the source. Add them only when they work. If a repository is private, the form lets you mark its visibility so the entry is clear about it.

### Icon

The optional icon is decorative. Use it for visual consistency if you like it, and leave it out otherwise.

## Writing it well: before and after

Strong project entries separate your contribution from the team's and name a result.

**Before:** Made a website for a restaurant using HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

**After:** Booking site for a 40-seat restaurant (Jane Doe, personal project). Built a table-reservation form with availability checking in React and Node.js, replacing phone bookings for roughly a third of reservations in the first two months.

The first entry says what technologies were used. The second says who it was for, what was built, and what changed because of it.

Here is a group project, where the main risk is claiming the whole thing.

**Before:** We developed an inventory management system for a university course.

**After:** Inventory tracker for a university software engineering course, team of four. I designed the database schema and built the stock-adjustment API in Python and Flask, with automated tests covering 85 percent of the endpoints.

The second version says "I" where it matters, and gives the reader something to ask about in an interview.

A non-technical example shows that the method is the same.

**Before:** Ran a social media campaign for a local charity.

**After:** Planned a six-week awareness campaign for a food-bank charity, writing and scheduling 30 posts and a short video series. Followers grew from 600 to 1,900, and volunteer sign-ups rose by 22 percent over the period.

Even without code, the entry has a scale, an action and an outcome.

## Variations by stage and situation

**Students and recent graduates.** Projects can lead your CV when you have little paid experience. Choose course projects, a thesis, hackathons and personal builds, and be honest about whether each was solo or in a team. Include a link where possible.

**Career changers.** Use projects to prove skills your job titles do not show. Build or document two or three pieces of work in the new field and describe them with the same discipline as a job.

**Experienced professionals.** Keep projects short, and use them for work that is not already covered by your roles, such as open source, a talk, a published tool or a side business. If your jobs already describe your achievements, the projects section can shrink to a line or two, or disappear.

**Designers and creatives.** Describe the brief, your role, the process and the result, and link to a portfolio instead of attaching images to the CV. A project entry here is a summary that leads to the full case study.

**Open source contributors.** Name the project, say what you contributed, and give a figure such as accepted pull requests or issues resolved. Link to your merged work, not just to your profile.

**Freelancers.** Treat each notable client engagement as a project, and describe it without confidential details. A short list of three or four such entries is often stronger than a long list of clients.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is listing tutorial projects as if they were original work. Following a course is fine for learning, but a reviewer who recognises the same to-do app from a hundred applications learns nothing. Change it, extend it, or leave it out.

Another is describing the technology and not the outcome. A reader wants to know why the project mattered, not only which framework you picked.

Overclaiming is a third. If you wrote a part of a group project, say so. Interviewers often ask about the details, and a mismatch is worse than modesty.

Dead links are a fourth. A repository that returns an error, or a demo that no longer loads, makes the entry look careless. Check each link before you send the CV.

Finally, too many entries dilute the section. Pick the best few, order them by relevance to the job, and let the rest go.

## A final check

For each entry, check that a stranger could explain what it was, what you did and what resulted from it. Then check that the tools you list are ones you could talk about for five minutes. If an entry fails either test, rewrite it or remove it.

Projects work best when they match the role. Read the posting again, find the two requirements that matter most, and put the project that proves them first.
