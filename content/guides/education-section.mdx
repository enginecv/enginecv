The education section is short on most CVs, but it does a specific job. It confirms that you meet any formal requirement for the role, it explains your starting point if you are early in your career, and it gives context for everything that follows. A reader should be able to take it in within a few seconds.

This guide goes through each field in the editor's education form, then shows how the section should change depending on where you are in your career. All names and institutions below are invented.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

Recruiters check education for three things: whether you hold the qualification the posting asks for, whether it comes from a recognisable institution, and when you finished. Some roles filter on a minimum level, such as a bachelor's degree, so being easy to parse matters.

An applicant tracking system reads this section into structured fields: institution, degree type, field of study and dates. If you write a degree in a creative way, or merge everything into one long line, the parser may miss the level or the subject. Use the standard names for qualifications and keep one entry per programme.

The second job of the section is to show relevance. A degree in a closely related field helps. A degree in an unrelated field is still useful as evidence of ability to study and finish, but then the supporting detail should point toward the role you want.

## Field by field

### Degree or qualification

In the form this is the degree type, such as Bachelor of Science, Master of Arts or Diploma. Write the full name the first time, in the form the institution uses. Abbreviations such as BSc and MA are fine when the reader will surely know them, but avoid local abbreviations that an international reader might not recognise.

### Field of study

Name the subject exactly, for example Computer Science or Nursing, not a vague umbrella. If your programme had a specialisation that matters for the job, add it after the subject, such as Computer Science with a focus on data engineering.

### Institution

Give the official name of the school, college or university. If it is not widely known outside its country, add the country in the location field so an international recruiter can place it. The optional link field is helpful for programme pages, as long as the page is still live.

### Location

City and country are enough. Leave this out only if the institution name already contains it.

### Start and end dates

Use month and year, or just the years if you prefer a cleaner look, and be consistent across entries. For a programme still in progress, put an expected end date or use the present, and say so in the description, for example expected graduation in June.

### Grade or score

The score field holds a grade point average, a classification or a final mark. Include it only if it is strong relative to what the reader expects, and always in a form they can interpret. If your country uses an unusual scale, state the scale once, such as 4.6 out of 5.

### Courses

A short list of relevant modules works well when you have little work experience. Pick four to six that match the job, not your whole transcript. Leave the list out once your career has spoken for itself.

### Description

Use this for a thesis title, a notable project, honours, a scholarship, or a leadership role in a student society. Keep it to a line or two. If you are a recent graduate, this is where you can borrow from the work experience style and show results.

## Writing it well: before and after

The difference between a weak and a strong entry is usually specificity and relevance, not length.

**Before:** University of Northfield, studied business, graduated 2021.

**After:** BSc Business Administration, University of Northfield, 2018 to 2021. Final-year project on supplier cost analysis for a local manufacturer, graded 78 percent.

The first version leaves out the degree type, the dates and any detail. The second states the qualification, the span and one piece of evidence about the work.

Here is an example for an early-career applicant with a technical focus.

**Before:** Bachelor of Engineering, various subjects including programming and electronics.

**After:** BEng Electronic Engineering, Eastmoor University, 2020 to 2024. Relevant modules: embedded systems, signal processing, control theory. Thesis: a low-power sensor node using a 32-bit microcontroller.

The first entry lists vague subjects. The second names the actual modules and a thesis, which tells a hiring manager exactly where your knowledge is.

A third example shows how to handle studies that did not end in a degree.

**Before:** Dropped out of university.

**After:** Studied Mechanical Engineering at Westfield Institute, 2016 to 2018. Completed 120 of 240 credits before moving into full-time work as a maintenance technician.

The honest version explains how far you got and why you moved on, in neutral language that invites no further questions.

## Variations by stage and situation

**Students and recent graduates.** Lead with education and give it room. Add relevant modules, projects, a thesis, honours, exchange semesters and society roles. Keep it to the strongest items so it does not read as padding.

**Early career, one to three years in.** Keep education above or just below your first jobs, with the degree, the institution and a single line of detail. Drop the modules list once you have work results to show.

**Experienced professionals.** Reduce each entry to degree, subject, institution and years. Remove the grade, the modules and the description. The reader cares much more about your recent roles.

**Career changers.** If your degree is unrelated, keep the entry short and add a recent course, certificate or project that connects you to the new field, in the certificates or courses sections.

**Applying internationally.** Add the country, and when an employer may not know your qualification, add a short equivalence note in the description, such as the level on a national framework. Do not invent an equivalence you cannot support.

**Ongoing studies.** List the programme with its expected end date and say that it is in progress. If you are studying alongside work, mention the part-time or evening format, as it shows commitment.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is giving the section too much space. A paragraph on every module from a degree from ten years ago pushes better material off the page.

A close second is missing the basics: no degree type, no dates, or an institution name that cannot be found. These are the fields recruiters and parsers look for first.

Another mistake is listing a degree in progress as complete. Always make the status clear, because it is easy to verify.

Inconsistent order is another easy error. Use reverse chronological order, newest first, unless you have a strong reason to put the most relevant qualification at the top.

Finally, many people include secondary school long after it stopped being relevant. If you hold a higher qualification, the reader already knows you finished school.

## A final check

Read each entry and check that a stranger could answer four questions: what qualification, in what subject, from which institution and when. If any answer is missing, add it. Then decide whether each extra line, such as a grade or a module list, helps with the job in front of you. If not, cut it.

Education sets the frame, and your work experience, skills and summary do the persuading. Once this section is clean, move on to the parts that carry more weight.
