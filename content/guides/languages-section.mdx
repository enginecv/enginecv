A languages section looks small, but it can decide an application. For international employers, public bodies, healthcare, customer support and many technical roles, the languages you work in are part of the job requirements, not a nice extra.

This guide covers each field in the editor's language form, the choice between a single level and a per-skill grid, how to show proof, and how to decide what to include. For a full explanation of what each level means and how exams map to them, read our guide to [CEFR language levels](/blog/cefr-language-levels-on-your-cv). The example people and organisations here are invented.

## What recruiters and ATS look for

A recruiter checks two things: whether you cover the languages the role needs, and whether the level is believable. A posting that asks for "business English and conversational German" can be answered with two lines, as long as they use the same terms.

Applicant tracking systems match the language name and often the level, so write names in their standard form and use the level scale the employer uses. Where a posting uses CEFR, use CEFR. Where it uses words like "fluent", give the nearest CEFR level and a word, so both kinds of search can find you.

Readers also check consistency. If your CV says you are at the top level in a language but your experience, education and interview suggest otherwise, they trust the rest of the document less.

## Field by field

### Language

Enter the language name as speakers of the language and employers know it, for example German, not Deutsch, unless the posting writes it that way. If you speak a regional variety that matters for the job, such as Brazilian Portuguese or Swiss German, name it.

### Proficiency level

This is the single overall level: a CEFR level, or native for your first language. Choose the level you can actually sustain on a normal working day, not the best day you have had. If your abilities differ strongly, switch to the per-skill option described below.

### Assessment

The assessment setting says how the level was established. You can leave it blank, mark it as self-assessed, or mark it as issued by an organisation. For native speakers the form hides it, since it does not apply.

### Issuing organisation

For an assessed level, name the body that issued the result, such as an examination centre, a national institute or a testing company. Use the name an employer would recognise.

### Assessment ID

If the exam gives a certificate or result number, add it here. Include it only when the issuer expects it to be quoted for verification, and leave it out if it reveals anything private.

### Verification link

Some issuers offer a page where anyone can confirm a result. If yours does, paste the link, because it turns a claim into something a reader can check in seconds. If the link needs a login or expires, leave it out.

### Per-skill levels

The detailed option lets you rate listening, reading, spoken interaction, spoken production and writing separately and can show them as a grid. Use it when the levels differ by more than a step, when a posting stresses one skill, or when you hold an exam that reports results per skill.

### Description

Use the description sparingly, for context that a level cannot carry: how you use the language at work, the domain vocabulary you know, or the period you lived abroad. One line is enough.

## Writing it well: before and after

Good language entries are consistent, specific and proportionate.

**Before:** English: fluent. German: good. Bosnian: native.

**After:** Bosnian, native. English, C1, used daily in an international engineering team. German, B2 (Goethe-Zertifikat B2, 2025), reading at C1.

The first version gives vague words that cannot be compared. The second gives levels, a use case and a certificate, and it shows where skills differ.

A second example shows how to present a self-assessed level without overreaching.

**Before:** Spanish: advanced.

**After:** Spanish, B1 (self-assessed). Hold everyday conversations and read news articles. Currently preparing for a B2 exam in autumn.

The honest version states the level and what it means in practice, and it adds a goal. A reader who tests it in an interview will find it accurate.

A third example is a per-skill profile where speaking lags behind.

**Before:** French: C1.

**After:** French: reading C1, writing B2, listening B2, spoken interaction B1, spoken production B1. Mostly work with French documentation and written correspondence.

The grid shows a real strength and a real limit, and it points the reader at the kind of work you can do now.

## Variations by stage and situation

**Students and recent graduates.** Languages can add weight when your experience is short. Include exams you have passed, exchange semesters and any study in another language, and name the level for each.

**Multilingual professionals.** List the languages in the order the job needs them, not alphabetically. Put the ones named in the posting first, then the rest in order of strength.

**Roles that serve customers or patients.** Mention the settings where you use each language, such as phone support or patient intake. A level plus a context is more convincing than a level alone.

**Translators and interpreters.** Use the per-skill detail and name the direction of your work, such as translation from German into English, plus any certification from a professional body.

**Heritage speakers.** If you grew up speaking a language at home but never studied it formally, say so. You may be native in speech and lower in writing, and the per-skill grid lets you show that without losing credit for what you can do.

**Applying internationally.** Check which scale the employer or country uses. If it is not CEFR, give the closest level and the name of the exam on its own scale, and never present a conversion you cannot support.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is inflating a level. Interviews are often held partly in a listed language, and a gap between claim and ability is noticed within minutes.

The second is listing a language you could not use at work. A phrase-book level adds little, and a very long list can make the strong entries harder to find.

A third is mixing scales. A CV that says "C1" for one language, "advanced" for another and "3 out of 5" for a third makes comparison impossible. Pick CEFR and use it throughout.

Another is forgetting to update. A level you set five years ago may no longer be true, up or down, and an old exam result may be out of date for an employer's rules. Review the section whenever you edit the CV.

Finally, do not hide the language the posting asks for. If you hold it at a modest level, say so honestly and add how you plan to improve it, because being clear is better than being absent.

## Keeping the section honest over time

Language ability moves in both directions. If you use a language every day at work, it will probably improve past the level you wrote down years ago. If you studied one at school and rarely touch it, it will fade. Review each entry whenever you update the CV, and change the level to match what you could do today.

A quick test helps. Pick a short article in the language and see whether you follow it without help, then try writing a few paragraphs about your work. If either feels hard, the level on your CV is probably a step too high.

For languages you want to grow, a small plan is worth a line. Mention the course you are taking or the exam you are booked for, and the reader sees direction as well as a level.

## A final check

Read your list as a hiring manager would and ask: do I cover every language the posting names, is each level stated on the same scale, and could I defend each one in a conversation of five minutes? Remove or rewrite whatever fails.

Then check the order and the proof. Put the strongest and most relevant language first, and add a certificate or a verification link wherever you can. Short, precise and checkable beats long and vague.
