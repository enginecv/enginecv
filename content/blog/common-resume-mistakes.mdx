Most resume problems are not about talent. They are editing problems. Candidates often have useful experience, but the document either buries it, overstates it, or makes it harder to trust.

If you want a faster improvement loop, stop looking for magic phrases and start removing the patterns that create doubt.

This guide goes through nine of the most common patterns, with a before and after for the ones that are easiest to fix. The examples are invented, so replace the details with your own work. None of it needs a special template.

## Mistake 1: Writing bullets that say nothing

Bullets like `worked on`, `helped with`, or `responsible for` usually tell the reader very little. They signal activity without showing contribution.

Better bullets usually answer at least one of these questions:

- What changed because of your work?
- How big was the scope?
- What system, tool, or process did you own?
- What evidence makes the claim believable?

Here is the difference in practice.

```text
Responsible for customer support and reporting.
```

```text
Answered about 60 customer emails a day in a team of 20 and wrote the weekly report the support lead used to plan shifts.
```

The second version gives the scale, shows what you did yourself and says who used the result. None of that needed a dramatic achievement, only a closer look at the work.

If you cannot find a number, use another kind of evidence. How often did the task happen? How many people relied on it? How long did it take before and after you changed it? Which problem stopped coming back? Any of these makes a claim easier to check than an adjective does.

## Mistake 2: Chasing keywords without context

Keyword alignment matters, but stuffing terms into unrelated sections makes the resume weaker.

If the job description mentions `stakeholder management`, `Python`, and `forecasting`, those words should appear where they are true. Do not turn the skills section into a pile of search terms with no supporting evidence.

Job descriptions are a useful guide to the words a hiring team uses, and some recruiters and tracking systems do search for them, although how much varies from one company to the next. A practical routine is to read the advert, note the five or six terms that repeat, and then check each one you can honestly claim. It should appear in the place where the work happened, usually inside an experience bullet, and not only in a list.

## Mistake 3: Using a format that fights the content

Some resumes look polished but make basic information harder to find. A recruiter should not need to decode the structure before they can assess your experience.

- Keep your headline readable.
- Keep date placement consistent.
- Keep section names standard.
- Keep the page visually calm.

Watch for the usual offenders: two or three columns where the reading order is unclear, progress bars or star ratings for skills, icons that replace words, and text placed inside images. A bar that says `Python 80%` also raises an awkward question, because nobody can say what 80% means.

A simple test helps. Copy the whole document and paste it into a plain text editor. If the text comes out in a sensible order, both people and software are likely to read it correctly. ## Mistake 4: Treating every experience entry equally

Not every role deserves the same amount of space.

Your most recent and most relevant experience should carry the narrative. Older roles can be compressed once the reader already understands your direction.

As a rough guide, give the current or latest role the most bullets, give the one before it fewer, and reduce anything older to a line with the title, the employer and the dates. A job from many years ago in an unrelated field can often be one line, or grouped under a heading such as `Earlier experience`. Shortening a role is not hiding it, as long as the dates stay accurate.

## Mistake 5: Leaving gaps and inconsistencies unexplained

Overlapping dates, months that do not add up, or a gap with no explanation make a reader stop and wonder. A gap is not a problem in itself. People take time for study, caring for family, moving country, illness and job searches.

A short, plain note is usually enough: `Parental leave`, `Relocation`, `Full-time study`. Do not stretch dates to hide a gap, because dates are easy to compare against a LinkedIn profile or a reference, and a mismatch costs far more trust than the gap would have. Use one date format throughout, for example `03/2022 to 08/2024`, and do not switch between months and years only.

## Mistake 6: Sending the same resume to every job

Tailoring does not mean rewriting everything. Keep one complete version that holds all your experience, then make a copy for each application and change only what helps that role.

- Move the most relevant bullets to the top of each entry.
- Adjust the headline so it names the role you are applying for.
- Make sure the words from the advert that are true for you appear where they belong.
- Cut the lines that do not help this application.

## Mistake 7: Inflating the claims

Rounding up a title, presenting a team's result as your own, or listing a tool you opened once costs more than it gains. Interviews and reference checks tend to test exactly these claims, and one that falls apart casts doubt on the rest of the page.

Be precise about your part. If you contributed to a project, say what you contributed. `Contributed to the migration by writing and testing the data export scripts` is stronger than `Led the migration` when you did not lead it, and it is the version you can answer questions about.

## Mistake 8: Skills that float free of the work

A long skills list with no link to any work gives the reader nothing to check. Wherever you can, attach a skill to the role or project where you used it, because a skill that appears next to real work is easier to believe. Some readers do scan the list first, so a short one still has a place. Do both: a compact list, plus the same skills named inside the experience entries.

Self-rated levels and skill bars are subjective. One person's `expert` is another person's `intermediate`, and a high level invites questions that test it. Either leave levels out, or claim only what you could defend if someone asked you a detailed question about it.

## Mistake 9: Small errors that undermine care

Typos, inconsistent capitalisation, a company name left over from the last application you tailored, a phone number with a missing digit. Each one is small, and together they suggest the document was not checked.

- Name the file clearly, for example `Jana-Novak-CV.pdf`, not `final_v7_NEW.pdf`.
- Send the file to yourself and click every link in it.
- Check that your email address and phone number are correct and written the same way everywhere.
- Read the CV aloud once. Your ear finds repeated words and clumsy sentences that your eyes skip.

## The final review pass

A strong final pass is usually simple:

1. Read the resume out loud once.
2. Remove anything you cannot defend in an interview.
3. Check that the first half of page one makes the target role obvious.
4. Confirm that every section supports the job you want next.
5. Open the exported file, not the editor, and check the page breaks and links.
6. Show it to one person outside your field and ask what job they think you want. If the answer is wrong, the headline and summary need work.

That pass often matters more than adding another design flourish or another line of soft skills.
