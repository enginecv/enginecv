If you apply for a job in Germany, Austria or Switzerland, you will usually send a cover letter, called an *Anschreiben* or *Motivationsschreiben*, together with your *Lebenslauf*. The letter is often judged on its content, but also on whether it looks like a letter written by someone who knows local conventions.

German business letters follow a national standard, DIN 5008, which fixes where the blocks sit on the page. Most readers cannot quote it, yet they notice when a letter ignores it, in the same way a reader in the United States notices a letter in an unusual format. This article explains the layout, the wording that goes with it and the mistakes to avoid.

## What is different from an English letter

The content principles are the same: say why you are a fit, give evidence, show you know the employer. If you need a general method, read [how to write a cover letter that gets read](/blog/how-to-write-a-cover-letter). What changes in Germany is the form.

- **Formality.** The register is more formal than in many English-speaking workplaces, even for modern companies. You use *Sie* for the reader, not *du*, unless the posting itself uses *du*.
- **Structure.** A subject line stands above the salutation and names the position.
- **Layout.** The address of the recipient sits in a fixed place on the page, so that it shows through the window of a standard envelope.
- **Length.** One page is the norm. Two is rare.

None of this is a trick. It is a shared convention that makes letters easy to file and read.

## DIN 5008 in plain terms

DIN 5008 is a German standard for writing and layout of text, including business letters. It describes margins, zones and typographic details so that letters look alike and fit window envelopes.

For a one-page application on A4 paper, the points that matter are these.

- **Margins.** Text starts 25 mm from the left edge and stops 20 mm from the right edge.
- **Letterhead zone.** At the top of the page, above the address, is room for the sender's details. In an application, that is your name and contact information.
- **Address field.** The recipient's address goes in a block 85 mm wide and 45 mm high, which starts 45 mm from the top edge. The first 5 mm are a narrow strip for a small return-address line, and the following 40 mm hold the recipient's name and address.
- **Information block.** To the right of the address field you may put your date and, if relevant, a reference. Many applications simply place the place and date here.
- **Subject line.** After the address field and a gap of about two blank lines comes the subject line. It is written in plain text without the word "Betreff" in current practice, and often in bold.
- **Fold and punch marks.** Short marks at the left edge at 105 mm and 210 mm from the top show where to fold the sheet in three, and a mark at 148.5 mm shows the centre for punching holes.

The numbers come from the standard as described in public sources, and the editions differ slightly. Older editions started the address field a little lower. If you plan to print a letter for a window envelope, print one copy first, fold it and check that the address sits properly in the window.

## Block by block

Here is what goes where on a typical application letter.

### Sender

Your full name, street and number, postcode and city, then phone and email. Many people put this block at the top of the page. In the strict layout, a one-line version of your name and address also appears in the small strip above the recipient's address, so that it is visible in the window.

### Recipient

The company name first, then the department or contact person if you have one, then street and postcode with city. Titles such as *Frau* and *Herr* appear on the line before the name when you know it. Do not add the country for a domestic letter.

### Place and date

Written as "Berlin, 19. November 2026" or in the numeric form "19.11.2026". The day comes first and the month is a word or number, never the American order.

### Subject line

The subject line tells the reader at a glance what the letter is about. For applications it names the job, for example "Bewerbung als Datenanalyst (m/w/d)". If the posting contains a reference number, add it to the subject. The tag "(m/w/d)" is common in German postings, and copying the exact title from the posting is safe.

### Salutation

Use the name when you have it: "Sehr geehrte Frau Müller," or "Sehr geehrter Herr Schmidt,". If you do not have a name, "Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren," is the standard. A comma follows the salutation and the first sentence starts with a lowercase letter, unless it begins with a noun or a name.

### Body

The body has the same job as in any cover letter: an opening that names the position and a reason to read on, a paragraph of evidence, a paragraph about the employer and a short close. In German, it is normal to state your earliest start date or notice period in the closing, since employers plan around it.

### Closing and signature

"Mit freundlichen Grüßen" is the standard closing, without a comma after it. Leave space for a handwritten signature if you print the letter, then type your name below. For a PDF application, a typed name is acceptable, and some people add an image of a signature.

### Enclosures

You may add the word "Anlagen" at the bottom with a short list of what you attach, such as the CV and certificates. It is optional but common.

## Wording that sounds right

A direct translation of an English letter often sounds odd in German. A few adjustments help.

- Avoid the long English opening about excitement. German readers prefer a factual first sentence that states the position and your relevant experience.
- Keep sentences shorter than German grammar tempts you to write them. A letter with three-line sentences is tiring to read.
- Use concrete achievements and figures. The German market likes proof more than enthusiasm.
- State what you can do for the company, not only what you want from the job.
- Avoid phrases that have lost their meaning, such as "hiermit bewerbe ich mich", unless your field expects them.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing "Betreff:"** before the subject. It is no longer part of current practice.
- **Putting a comma after "Mit freundlichen Grüßen".**
- **Using the US date order or month-first formats.**
- **Printing on US Letter paper.** German offices use A4. If your document is set up for Letter, content drifts and the address may miss the window.
- **Starting the salutation with "Hallo"** in a formal application. It can work in a start-up that writes casually, but do not guess.
- **Forgetting the gender-neutral tag** when you copy the job title from the posting.

## Matching the Lebenslauf

In Germany the CV and the cover letter are read as one application. The letter should use the same name and contact details as your Lebenslauf, and the same typography. A tidy, matching pair is a quiet signal of care. If your CV has a photo, which is still common in Germany, decide deliberately whether the letter should show it too. Most letters leave it out, because the photo is already on the CV.
