A job search produces paper. Even when it is all digital, the pile grows: a CV in two languages, a letter for each application, a copy of each posting, a note about who replied. After a few weeks it is hard to say which letter went to which company, or which version of your phone number each one carried.

The fix is not a better folder name. It is a clearer relationship between the documents. This article explains one that works well in practice: every cover letter belongs to exactly one CV, and one CV can have as many letters as you need. It also covers what happens when that CV changes, what to do with a letter after you send it, and how to handle a CV you no longer want.

## Two documents, one relationship

A CV and a cover letter are different kinds of document with different lifespans.

- A **CV** is long-lived. You build it once, improve it over months and reuse it for many applications. You might have a few of them: one per language, one per kind of role.
- A **cover letter** is short-lived. It is written for one posting, sent once and rarely used again except as a starting point.

If you think about how the two relate, one rule falls out of it. A letter that follows a CV needs one to take its details from, so a linked letter has exactly one CV. (You can also write a letter with no CV at all, in which case you type the sender details yourself. This article is about the linked kind.) A CV is used for many applications, so a CV has many letters. In database language that is a one-to-many relationship, and in everyday terms it means three practical things.

1. **A letter never points at two CVs.** There is no sensible answer to "whose phone number is this" if there are two.
2. **A CV can collect as many letters as you write.** Twenty applications with the same CV means twenty letters under that CV.
3. **A letter can move.** If you decide that an application should go out with your German CV after all, the letter changes its CV. It does not need to be rewritten.

The first rule is the one that keeps the system honest. The second is what makes it useful. The third covers the way real searches change direction.

## Why not a separate letter for each CV copy?

Many people handle this by copying the CV for every application and writing the letter next to it. It works at first. Then the problems start.

You spot a typo in your CV and fix it in one copy. The other copies still carry it. You change your email address and have to remember how many files hold the old one. You tweak a bullet after an interview and cannot say which applications went out with the earlier wording.

Copying turns every correction into a search. Linking turns it into a single edit: the CV holds the facts, and the letters read them from there. When your phone number changes, you change it once, and every letter that has not been sent yet shows the new number.

## What a letter should read from the CV

Not everything in a CV belongs in a letter. These are the things that should come from it, because they should always match.

- **Your name and contact details.** The sender block of the letter should be the same as the CV header, character for character.
- **Your signature name.** It should be spelled the way your CV spells it.
- **The design.** Fonts, accent colour and header style tell a recruiter that two pages belong together.
- **A few facts you refer to in the text,** such as your current job title, your location or your strongest skills.

What should not come from the CV is the text of the letter itself. A recruiter sees your name and number at the top, but the argument of the letter is yours, written for one job. Editing the CV must never rewrite a sentence that you wrote for a particular employer.

## When the CV changes: draft versus sent

Here is the question that decides whether this approach is safe: what should a letter do when its CV changes?

The answer depends on whether you have sent it.

**A draft should follow.** It is unfinished, and you want it to be right when you send it. If you fix your email address today, the draft written last week should carry the fix.

**A sent letter should hold still.** A sent letter is a record of what a recruiter received. If your CV changes next month, that record must not change with it. Otherwise you could open an old application, see today's phone number and design on it, and believe that is what you sent.

So a good system needs a deliberate step between the two, and that step is "mark as sent". At that moment the letter keeps the sender details, the design and the other facts it used, and stops following the CV. If you need to change it afterwards, you either reopen it on purpose and accept that it follows the CV again, or you duplicate it and work on the copy.

This distinction is more important than it seems. It is what lets you look back at an application six months later and know what was actually in it.

## Writing the next letter from the last one

Most people write their second letter by copying their first. That is a good habit, with one condition: you must change everything that is specific to the first employer.

A duplicate of a letter should keep your body text and stay linked to the same CV, so you start with a working draft. It is your job to review the parts that are bound to the old job.

- The company name, the recipient and the role.
- The opening line that referred to the old posting.
- The paragraph about why you want to work at that company.
- Any detail that only made sense for the earlier role.

A short list of these items, kept next to your base letter, prevents the most embarrassing mistake in a job search: sending the right letter to the wrong company.

Paragraphs you reuse often, such as the closing about availability, can be saved separately and dropped into any letter as a copy. Keep them independent of the letters they came from, so that improving a saved paragraph does not secretly change an application you already sent.

## Moving a letter between CVs

Sometimes a letter belongs under a different CV. You wrote it for an English application, then a similar role appears in Germany. Or you built a second CV aimed at a different kind of job and want an earlier letter to go with it.

Two operations cover this.

- **Re-pointing** moves the letter to another CV. The text stays and the sender block and the design follow the new CV.
- **Duplicating into another CV** leaves the original where it was and creates a copy under the other CV.

Choose re-pointing when the letter should leave its old CV for good. Choose duplicating when both should exist. In either case, the body of the letter is yours and does not change; only the parts that came from the CV do.

If you changed anything in the sender block for one application, such as a work address, check it after the move. A value you set for one letter should be visible to you as an exception, not something that quietly disappears or spreads to other letters.

## Deleting a CV that has letters

CVs get retired. You merge two of them, or you drop one that no longer fits. The risk is that deleting a CV can quietly delete or break the letters attached to it.

A safe rule is that deleting a CV never deletes a letter as a side effect. For every letter attached to it, there are three cases.

1. **Sent letters** already carry their own snapshot of the sender details and design. They stay readable and exportable whatever happens to the CV.
2. **Drafts you still want to finish** can be attached to another CV, and then they follow that one.
3. **Drafts you want to keep as they are** can be converted into a frozen snapshot of their last state, so they stay readable without a CV behind them.

In every case, you should be told how many letters are affected before anything is removed, and you should be able to cancel. A person who deletes a CV to tidy up must never find out three weeks later that an application they wanted to reread is gone.

## Keeping a long search tidy

Once the relationship is clear, a few habits keep a big search under control.

- **Group by CV, then by status.** Drafts you are still working on come first, sent letters form your history.
- **Name each letter by company and role,** so that a list of twenty is searchable at a glance.
- **Mark a letter as sent the moment it leaves.** It takes a second and makes the record trustworthy.
- **Retire CVs deliberately.** Decide for each letter what happens to it before you remove its CV.
- **Back up regularly.** If everything lives on your device, export your data now and then, with letters, links and saved paragraphs included.
