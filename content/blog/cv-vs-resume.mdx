Ask ten people what the difference between a CV and a resume is and you will get several confident, conflicting answers. That is because the honest answer depends on where you live and who is reading.

In some countries the two words mean the same thing. In others they name two different documents with different lengths, contents and purposes. If you apply across borders, or you are not sure what a job posting expects, a wrong guess costs you a rejection for something that has nothing to do with your skills.

This guide explains what the words usually mean, where the differences are real, and how to decide quickly which one to send.

## The short version

- In the United States and Canada, a **resume** is a short, targeted summary of your work history for a specific job. A **CV** is a longer, complete record, used mostly for academic, research and medical roles.
- In the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and most of Europe, **CV** is the normal word for the document you send when you apply for almost any job. The word "resume" is rarely used.
- In continental Europe the same document goes by local names, such as `Lebenslauf` in German, `curriculum vitae` or `CV` in French, `currículum` in Spanish and `životopis` in Bosnian, Croatian and Serbian.

So the practical question is not "which of the two is correct". It is "what does this employer, in this country, mean by the word they used".

## What a resume usually is

A resume, in the sense used in North America, is a **marketing document**. It does not try to tell the whole story. It selects the parts of your history that matter for one job and leaves the rest out.

Typical traits:

- One page for most people, two for someone with a long, relevant career.
- Reverse chronological, with your most recent role first.
- Bullets that describe what you did and what changed because of it.
- Tailored to the posting. You may remove older or unrelated jobs completely.
- A short summary at the top, a skills list, work experience and education.
- No personal details beyond contact information, and usually no photo, date of birth or marital status.

The point is speed. A recruiter reading a stack of applications should understand in seconds who you are and why you fit.

## What a CV usually is

When the word CV is used in the North American sense, it is a **complete record** of your professional and academic life. Nobody cuts it down for each application. It grows as you do.

Typical traits:

- No page limit. Two pages is short, and an established researcher's CV can run to ten or more.
- Covers publications, conference talks, grants, teaching, supervision, awards, memberships and sometimes the title of your thesis.
- Education often comes before work experience, especially early in an academic career.
- Written for readers who want depth and evidence: hiring committees, funding bodies, admissions panels.

The same CV may be sent to many places. Each reader looks for the parts that matter to them.

## What "CV" means in the UK, Europe and elsewhere

Outside North America, you should not read "CV" as "the long academic document". An employer in Dublin, Berlin or Sydney who asks for your CV usually wants what an American employer would call a resume: a clear summary of your work history and education, tailored to the job.

Common expectations, which vary by country and by industry:

- **Length**: often one to two pages. Longer is acceptable for senior or technical roles, but a reader still wants the strongest material near the top.
- **Order**: reverse chronological is the default.
- **Personal details**: practices differ. In Germany and Austria a photo and a tabular layout are traditional, though optional. In the United Kingdom and the United States, photos are normally left off.
- **Language**: write the CV in the language of the posting unless told otherwise.

Because customs differ, look at CVs from the country you are applying to. A few examples from people in your field and region teach you more than a general rule.

## Where Europass fits

Europass is a standard CV format promoted by the European Union. It has fixed sections, a language grid based on the CEFR levels (A1 to C2), and a layout many public institutions in Europe recognise.

You do not need Europass for most private-sector jobs, and many employers prefer a cleaner modern layout. Some public bodies, universities and EU programmes do ask for it or accept it. ## A quick way to decide which one to send

Work through these in order and stop at the first clear answer.

1. **Read the posting.** If it says "send your resume", "upload your CV" or names a format, follow it exactly. If it says "academic CV" or "full CV", send the long version.
2. **Check the type of role.** Academic, research, medical, scientific and some government roles want the complete record. Most business, technical and creative roles want the short version.
3. **Check the country.** North America: a resume for most jobs. Elsewhere: a CV that behaves like a resume, unless the role is academic.
4. **Check the channel.** An online form with fixed fields may import your document and then ask you to fix the result. Keep your formatting simple so the import works.
5. **If you still cannot tell**, send the short, targeted version. It is much easier to be asked for more detail than to be forgiven for sending eight pages when the reader wanted one.

## Adapting one document for different readers

You do not need two unrelated documents. Keep one complete master version and derive the others from it.

1. Write the **master** with everything: every job, project, publication, course and award, in full.
2. For a **resume-style application**, duplicate it. Remove what does not serve this job, cut bullets to the strongest three or four per role, and aim for one or two pages.
3. For an **academic CV**, duplicate the master again. Keep the detail, add the sections that matter in your field, and order them the way that field expects.
4. Keep a short note of which version went to which employer, so you send the right file again if they reply.

## Common mistakes

- **Sending an academic CV to a business job.** A reader who sees eight pages of publications for a marketing role concludes you did not read the posting.
- **Sending a one-page resume to an academic committee.** The committee wants to see the full evidence, and a short document looks thin.
- **Mixing conventions.** A photo, date of birth and marital status are normal in some countries and unwelcome in others. Follow the local norm for the market you are applying to.
- **Using the wrong word in the file name.** If the posting asks for a resume, `Jana-Novak-Resume.pdf` looks careful. It is a small detail, and it costs nothing.
- **Assuming a template decides the answer.** A template changes how a document looks. It does not change what belongs in it.
- **Translating word for word.** A CV written in German for a German employer is not a translation of an English resume. Section names, tone and ordering differ.

## What about ATS software?

Applicant tracking systems read your document into structured fields. Whether you call the document a CV or a resume makes no difference to the software. Clean structure helps: real text rather than images of text, standard section headings, a simple single-column layout and a file format the employer accepts.

No one outside the employer can promise that a particular layout will pass a particular system, so be wary of anyone who does. ## Quick answers

**Is a CV longer than a resume?** In North America, usually yes. Elsewhere, the two words usually describe the same document, so length depends on your experience and the role.

**Can I use the same file for both?** You can use the same content as a starting point. Edit it for the reader. A document built for a hiring manager in a hurry is not the same as one built for a research committee.

**Should I write CV or resume in my headline?** Neither. The document does not need a title that says what it is. Put your name at the top, and let the content speak.

**Does the word matter to a recruiter?** Rarely. What matters is that you gave them what they asked for, in a form they can read quickly.

## The takeaway

Treat "CV" and "resume" as labels that depend on the reader. Read the posting, check the country and the type of role, and when you are unsure, send the short, targeted version. Keep a complete master document so every other version is a careful edit, not a rewrite.
