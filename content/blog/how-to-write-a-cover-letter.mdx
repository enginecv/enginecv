Most cover letters are read in under a minute, and many are not read at all. Some employers skip them, some use them to decide between two close candidates, and some treat a missing letter as a sign that you did not care enough to apply properly. You cannot tell which kind you are dealing with, so the safe move is to write a letter that earns its place in under a minute.

This guide gives you a method that works for most jobs in most countries: a short letter in four paragraphs, built from facts you already have in your CV. It also covers the mistakes that make letters forgettable and a way to reuse your work for the next application without sending the same text twice.

## What a cover letter is for

Your CV is a record. It lists what you did, in a format that is easy to scan. A cover letter does something the CV cannot: it connects that record to one specific job, in sentences, in your own voice.

That gives the letter three jobs.

1. **Say why this job.** Not "I am looking for a new challenge", but something that could only be written about this employer.
2. **Pick the evidence.** Your CV holds everything. The letter chooses the two or three facts that matter most for this role and gives them room.
3. **Show how you communicate.** A reader learns more about how you write and think from one clear paragraph than from a list of skills.

A letter that only repeats the CV in longer sentences does none of these. If a sentence would still make sense in a letter to another company, it is probably filler.

## Keep it to one page

One page is the target in almost every market. That is roughly 250 to 350 words of body text, plus the sender block, the date, the recipient and the closing. Two pages is a sign that the letter is trying to be the CV.

Short does not mean thin. It means every paragraph has a reason to be there. If you are over the limit, cut the paragraph that explains your career in order, because the CV already does that.

## The four-paragraph structure

You do not need a clever structure. You need an order that a busy reader can follow without effort.

### Paragraph one: the reason you are writing

Open with the role you are applying for, where you found it if that is useful, and one sentence that says why you are a fit. Skip the opening about how excited you are to apply. The reader assumes you are interested because you sent the letter.

A good opening has a fact in it. Compare these two.

**Before:** I am writing to express my strong interest in the Data Analyst position advertised on your website, as I believe my skills and passion make me an ideal candidate.

**After:** I am applying for the Data Analyst role in your logistics team. For the past three years I have built the weekly delivery reports that a regional courier used to decide which routes to cut, and your posting describes the same problem at a larger scale.

The second version names a result, a kind of work and a link to the posting, all in two sentences.

### Paragraph two: your strongest evidence

Pick the one or two things from your CV that best match what the posting asks for, and tell them as a short story with a result. State the situation, what you did and what changed. Numbers help when you have them and are honest, but a clear outcome in words works too.

Do not list every skill. A paragraph that names eight tools reads like a keyword dump. Name the one or two that the employer asked for and show them in use.

### Paragraph three: why this employer

This is the paragraph most people skip, and the one that does the most to separate your letter from the pile. Say something specific you know about the company, its product, its customers or its way of working, and connect it to something you have done or want to do.

You do not need inside knowledge. A recent product launch, a project described on their site or a value they state in the posting is enough, as long as your sentence would not fit another company.

### Paragraph four: the close

End with what you would like to happen next and a plain thank you. Say you would be glad to talk, mention any practical detail that matters, such as a notice period, a relocation or a work permit, and stop. Do not apologise for taking their time and do not repeat your whole pitch.

## Choose the greeting with care

Address the letter to a named person when you can. Look at the posting, the company's team page or the recruiter's profile. If you cannot find a name after a few minutes of looking, use a neutral greeting that fits the language of the letter.

- English: "Dear Hiring Manager," is accepted nearly everywhere.
- German: "Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren," is the standard when you have no name.
- French: "Madame, Monsieur," is the safe form.
- Spanish and Bosnian have similarly formal defaults.

A wrong name is worse than no name. If a posting says to apply to a person, check that the name is current before you use it.

## Write like a person, not a template

Employers can recognise a template from the first line. The fix is not to add personality. It is to be specific and plain.

- **Use short sentences.** If you need to take a breath in the middle, split it.
- **Use verbs for what you did**, not adjectives for what you are. "Rebuilt the onboarding flow" says more than "dynamic and results-driven".
- **Cut hedges.** "I believe I could perhaps contribute" is weaker than "I can contribute".
- **Match the register of the posting.** A startup that writes casually does not need a formal letter. A law firm does.
- **Read it aloud.** Anything you stumble over gets rewritten.

## What to leave out

A cover letter is not the place for everything. These are common and avoidable.

- **A retelling of your whole career.** The CV does that.
- **Salary expectations,** unless the posting asks for them.
- **Negative remarks** about a past employer, even mild ones.
- **Explanations of gaps** in a long apology. If a gap needs context, one neutral sentence is enough.
- **Generic claims** such as "team player", "hard worker" or "fast learner" with nothing behind them.
- **Typos.** A letter is short, so a single mistake is very visible.

## Match the letter to the CV

The letter and the CV are read together, often within seconds of each other. They should look like they came from the same person on the same day: the same name and contact details, the same fonts, the same accent colour, the same level of formality. When the contact details differ between the two, a recruiter has to guess which is right. When the styles differ, the application looks assembled from parts.

That is a good reason to keep the two documents connected instead of maintaining two separate files. In EngineCV, [a cover letter is linked to one of your CVs](/blog/cover-letters-in-enginecv) and takes its sender block and design from it.

## Tailor in minutes, not hours

People who send many applications often stop tailoring because it feels like writing a new letter each time. You do not need to. Most of a letter stays the same from one application to the next, and the parts that change are small.

Keep a base letter and change three things for each job.

1. **The recipient, company and role.** Never forget one of these, because the wrong company name in a letter is an instant rejection.
2. **The opening hook.** One sentence connecting your experience to what this posting asks for.
3. **The employer paragraph.** Two or three sentences that are about this company only.

Everything else, such as the evidence paragraph for your strongest achievement and a standard closing about availability, can be reused with light edits. Keeping a small set of paragraphs you trust makes this much faster, and the [number of letters you keep per CV](/blog/one-cv-many-cover-letters) is worth a thought of its own.

## A short checklist before you send

Run through this list every time. It takes a minute and catches most of the problems that cost people interviews.

- Is the company name right, everywhere it appears?
- Is the role title the same as in the posting?
- Does the first paragraph say something that could only be written for this job?
- Is there one concrete result in the second paragraph?
- Would the third paragraph make sense in a letter to another company? If yes, rewrite it.
- Is it one page, in the format the employer asked for?
- Do the contact details match your CV exactly?
- Have you read it aloud once?
