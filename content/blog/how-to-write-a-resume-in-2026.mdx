Writing a resume in 2026 is less about clever formatting and more about clarity. Hiring teams still move quickly, many employers use applicant tracking systems to organise applications, and most strong resumes still win because they explain the candidate's value in plain language.

The safest starting point is a one-page or two-page document with predictable sections, clean dates, and bullet points that describe real outcomes. If a recruiter skims your file for ten seconds, they should still understand what you do, where you have done it, and why that work mattered.

## Before you write: choose a target and collect evidence

Most weak resumes were written without a target. Decide on one role you want next, and read three job adverts for it. Note the responsibilities and the terms that repeat. That gives you the reader's questions before you start answering them.

Then collect evidence in a separate document, not in the resume itself. For each job and project, write down what you were responsible for, the numbers you remember, the problems you solved, any feedback you received and anything you built or changed that is still in use. Most of it will not make the final cut. You are building a pool to choose from, and the best material is usually in the notes you would have forgotten.

## Start with the headline, not the objective

Old objective statements are rarely useful now. A short headline is better because it gives the reader context immediately.

Use a headline that answers one direct question: what role are you best suited for right now?

- `Frontend Engineer focused on performant React applications`
- `Operations Manager with experience scaling distributed support teams`
- `Junior Data Analyst with SQL, Excel, and dashboard reporting experience`

That line should connect naturally to your recent experience. If the headline promises one thing and the work history shows another, the resume feels generic.

## Add a short summary only if it earns its place

A summary of two or three lines can help, mainly when your direction is not obvious from the job titles. Say what you do, who you do it for and your strongest piece of proof. Skip phrases such as `hard-working team player` or `results-driven professional`, which could describe anyone and give the reader nothing to remember.

```text
Customer support lead with six years in SaaS, managing a team of eight and cutting average first-response time from five hours to one. Looking for an operations role.
```

If you cannot write a summary that says something specific, leave the section out. The headline and the first role will do the job.

## Focus each section on proof

Most weak resumes describe responsibilities. Strong resumes show evidence.

Instead of this:

```text
Responsible for managing client accounts and internal reporting.
```

Write this:

```text
Managed a portfolio of 24 client accounts and reduced monthly reporting time by 35 percent by standardizing dashboards and approval workflows.
```

The second version gives scale, ownership, and an outcome. That is what makes a bullet credible.

A useful formula is action, scope and result: what you did, how big it was, and what changed. It works across fields. `Trained 12 new hires on the booking system, so most could work unsupervised within two weeks` is a result for a hospitality supervisor. `Rewrote the intake form, which cut incomplete applications by about a third` is a result for an administrator.

When you do not have numbers, use another kind of proof: how often something happened, how many people depended on it, how long a task took before and after, or the fact that something you built is still in use. Avoid inventing precision. An honest `about a third` is better than a made-up `34.7 percent`.

## One experience entry, start to finish

Here is how the pieces fit together in a single role. The company and the numbers are invented.

```text
Support Team Lead, Example Software Ltd, 03/2021 to Present
- Lead eight support agents across two time zones; first-response time fell from five hours to one.
- Wrote 40 help-centre articles for the most common tickets; repeat contacts about password resets dropped by about a third.
- Run the weekly quality review, coaching each agent on five tickets.
```

Each bullet carries one idea, and each starts with a verb. The current role uses the present tense and a role that has ended uses the past tense. The bullets are ordered by relevance to the job being applied for, not by the date they happened. If the next job is a people-management role, the first bullet is the right one to lead with.

## Skills, tools and languages

List skills close to the work, as well as in a short skills section. A tool that appears inside the role where you used it is easier to believe than one that appears only in a list, although some readers scan the list first, so a compact one still helps. Keep it short and put what you use most first.

Be cautious with self-rated levels and skill bars. They are subjective, and they invite questions that test them. Either leave the levels out, or claim only what you could defend if someone asked you a detailed question.

For spoken languages, use the CEFR scale, A1 to C2, and name the exam if you have one. Our guide on [language levels on your CV](/blog/cefr-language-levels-on-your-cv) explains how to rate each skill honestly.

## Keep the structure ATS-friendly

An ATS-friendly resume is usually just a readable resume with consistent structure.

- Use standard section labels like Experience, Education, Skills, and Projects.
- Keep dates consistent across entries.
- Avoid tables for critical content.
- Avoid graphics that contain essential text.
- Use keywords that already belong to your experience instead of stuffing them everywhere.

This is also where it helps to compare your resume against the job description. If a role asks for stakeholder communication, process improvement, and SQL, those terms should show up naturally where they are true. ## Prioritize relevance over completeness

You do not need to tell your entire career story in equal depth.

Spend most of the space on the last five to ten years, the projects closest to the target role, and the accomplishments that are easiest to verify. Older or less relevant jobs can be shortened without hiding them.

On length, one page is a sensible aim early in a career, and two pages are reasonable when you have enough relevant evidence to fill them. Conventions differ by country and by field, so look at what people in your field and region usually send. Relevance matters more than the page count.

## Tailor without rewriting

Keep one complete version of your resume, and make a copy for each application. In the copy, move the most relevant bullets to the top, adjust the headline to the role, check that the terms from the advert that are true for you appear, and cut what does not help. ## Edit for scan speed

A resume that is technically correct can still fail because it is tiring to scan.

- Keep bullets short enough to read in one pass.
- Lead with strong verbs and concrete nouns.
- Remove filler words like `helped`, `worked on`, and `involved in` when they weaken ownership.
- Check that each section earns its space.

Finish by reading the whole document aloud, opening the exported file to check the page breaks and links, and asking one person to tell you in ten seconds what role you are aiming for.
