"Fluent", "good" and "basic" mean different things to different people. A recruiter in Munich reading "German: good" can't tell whether you can handle a phone call with a customer or only order lunch. That's why European employers expect language skills as **CEFR levels**: A1, A2, B1, B2, C1 and C2.

This guide explains what each level means, how to rate yourself without overselling or underselling, and how to write the result on your CV.

## What the six levels mean

The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) describes what you can **do** in a language, not how many years you studied it.

| Level | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| **A1** | You understand and use everyday phrases and can introduce yourself. |
| **A2** | You handle simple, routine tasks: shopping, directions, short messages. |
| **B1** | You cope with most everyday situations and can talk about work and familiar topics. |
| **B2** | You talk with native speakers without strain for either side and follow detailed discussions in your field. |
| **C1** | You use the language flexibly at work, write clear structured texts and rarely search for words. |
| **C2** | You understand practically everything and express yourself precisely, close to a native speaker. |

A and B levels are often split further (B1.1, B1.2) in course certificates. On a CV, the main level is enough.

## Rate each skill separately

Your skills are rarely the same level everywhere. Many people read German at B2 but speak at B1, or understand Bosnian perfectly but write it less confidently. The CEFR, and the Europass CV, therefore rate five skills:

- **Listening**: understanding speech, from slow and clear to fast, colloquial
- **Reading**: from short notices to long reports
- **Spoken interaction**: conversations, meetings, phone calls
- **Spoken production**: presenting, explaining, telling something on your own
- **Writing**: from short messages to formal letters and reports

Rating them separately is more honest, and it helps: an employer looking for someone who mainly reads documents cares less about your speaking level.

## How to rate yourself honestly

- **Use the descriptions, not your feelings.** Read the "can do" statement for each level and pick the highest one that is true on a normal day, not your best day.
- **Ask yourself about real situations.** Could you follow a team meeting? Write a complaint email without a translator? Explain a mistake to your manager?
- **Don't round up.** Interviews are often partly held in the language. A B1 who writes C1 loses trust in the first five minutes.
- **Don't undersell either.** Many people from the Balkans who grew up with German TV or worked in Germany underrate themselves.

If you want a structured check, the free [CEFR self-assessment](/tools/cefr-level) walks you through all five skills with the official descriptions and adds the result straight to a CV.

## A worked example

Here is how one person might rate their German, with the reasoning next to each skill. The example is invented, and the reasons are there to show the thinking, not to be copied.

| Skill | Level | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | B2 | Follows team meetings and the news, but loses track of fast regional dialects. |
| Reading | C1 | Reads contracts and technical documentation, with a dictionary only now and then. |
| Spoken interaction | B1 | Manages phone calls and small talk, but loses words in fast discussions. |
| Spoken production | B1 | Can give a short prepared presentation, but not improvise one. |
| Writing | B2 | Writes clear emails, with some grammar mistakes in formal letters. |

On a CV this person can write **German** – B2 overall (reading C1, speaking B1), or show all five skills in the grid. An employer reading it knows what to expect, and an interview in German is unlikely to contradict it.

## How certificates relate to the levels

A certificate makes your level easier to believe. The most common ones for German:

| Certificate | Levels | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Goethe-Zertifikat** | A1–C2 | One exam per level; the most widely known abroad. |
| **ÖSD** (Österreichisches Sprachdiplom) | A1–C2 | Austrian; widely accepted in Germany too. |
| **telc Deutsch** | A1–C2 | Common for integration courses and jobs. |
| **TestDaF** | about B2–C1 | Scored TDN 3–5: TDN 3 ≈ B2, TDN 5 ≈ C1. Mainly for university. |
| **DSH** | about B2–C2 | DSH-1 ≈ B2, DSH-2 ≈ C1, DSH-3 ≈ C2. Set by each university, so the mapping is approximate. |

For English, IELTS, TOEFL and Cambridge certificates map to CEFR levels the same way; their providers publish the conversion tables.

### When a certificate is old

A certificate shows your level on the day of the exam. Some employers, universities and authorities only accept certificates under a certain age, and the limit differs, so check the requirement before you rely on an old one.

If your certificate is a few years old and you have used the language at work since, say so next to it, for example **German** – B2 (Goethe-Zertifikat, 2021), used daily at work since. Do not raise the level on your own authority. If your skills have clearly improved, a new exam is the honest way to show it.

### Languages other than German and English

The CEFR is used for many languages, and the main exams for European languages usually report CEFR levels. Some exams use their own scales. When yours does, write the exam and the score, and add a CEFR level only if the exam provider publishes a conversion.

## How to write it on your CV

Keep it short and consistent. Put the language, the level, and the proof if you have it:

- **German** – B2 (Goethe-Zertifikat B2, 2025)
- **English** – C1
- **Bosnian** – native language

If your skills differ a lot, show the five skills in a small grid (the Europass layout) instead of one level. Write "native language" rather than "C2" for your mother tongue.

## What employers in Germany and Austria commonly expect

There is no single rule; it depends on the job and, for non-EU citizens, on the visa route. As a rough guide:

- **Office and customer-facing jobs** usually expect **B2** or higher in German.
- **Nursing**: recognition of your qualification in Germany generally requires **B2** German.
- **Doctors**: general German at **B2** plus a medical language exam (Fachsprachprüfung) oriented at **C1**.
- **IT and engineering** roles in international teams sometimes work in English, but German at B1 or higher still helps in interviews and daily life.

Visa rules change. Check the official sources for your situation: [Make it in Germany](https://www.make-it-in-germany.com/en/) for Germany and [migration.gv.at](https://www.migration.gv.at/en/) for Austria.

## Common mistakes

- **One level for a language when your skills differ a lot.** If reading is two levels above speaking, say so. It is more useful and more believable.
- **Writing "C2" for your mother tongue.** Use "native language", as above.
- **Inventing notation.** Writing "B2.5" or "strong B2" confuses a reader. The CEFR descriptors do include in-between steps such as B1+, but certificates and most employers work with the six main levels. Give the main level and describe the strength in words if you need to.
- **Listing a language you could not use at work.** A1 or A2 adds little to most applications. Include it when the language is relevant to the job, and rate it honestly.
- **Hiding the language the employer asked for.** Put the languages named in the advert first, then the rest.
- **Forgetting to update.** Levels change. Review them whenever you edit the CV.

## Talking about your level in an interview

Expect part of the interview to be held in a language you listed. Prepare a short introduction in it, and be ready for follow-up questions about your work. If a question is beyond your level, say so and answer more simply. That reads better than silence or a bluff, and it matches what your CV claimed.

## Quick checklist

- Each language has a CEFR level, not "good" or "fluent".
- Your mother tongue says "native language".
- Certificates are named with the year.
- Skills that differ a lot are shown per skill.
- The level matches what you can show in an interview.
