The hardest part of a first resume is the blank page under the heading "Work experience". It feels like proof that you have nothing to offer.

You do. A reader hiring for an entry-level role does not expect a long career. They want evidence that you can learn, finish things and behave reliably. That evidence exists in your coursework, your projects, the things you organised, the shifts you worked and the skills you taught yourself. Your job is to find it and describe it clearly, without inflating it.

This guide shows how to structure the document, where to look for material, how to write bullets that say something, and what to leave out. The examples are invented. Replace every detail with your own.

## What an employer looks for in a first resume

For an entry-level role or internship, a reader is usually asking a few simple questions:

- Can this person do the basics of the work, or learn them fast?
- Have they finished anything that took effort over weeks, not hours?
- Do they communicate clearly, in writing and in how they organised the document?
- Is there a reason to believe they will show up and care?

A neat one-page document that answers those questions beats a long one that pads. Short is not a weakness at this stage. Clear is the goal.

## Choose a structure that fits your situation

Without work history, the standard reverse chronological layout puts your thin section first. Change the order so your strongest material comes first.

A good default for a student or recent graduate:

1. **Name and contact details**
2. **A short summary** (two or three sentences)
3. **Education**
4. **Projects** (or coursework, or relevant activities)
5. **Skills**
6. **Experience**, if you have any part-time, seasonal or volunteer work
7. **Languages, certificates, awards**, if they apply

If you have a strong project or a relevant internship, put it above education. If your degree is the main qualification for the job, keep education on top. Pick whichever section would make a reader say "this person could do the job" first.

Aim for one page. If it spills onto a second page by a few lines, cut something rather than shrinking the font.

## Where to find material when you think you have none

Go through this list slowly and write down everything that applies. You will trim it later.

- **Education**: relevant courses, a thesis or final-year project, a strong grade if it helps you, scholarships, exchange semesters.
- **Projects**: anything you built, designed, wrote, analysed or organised. A website, a data analysis, a short film, a repair, a class project with a real outcome.
- **Part-time or casual jobs**: retail, hospitality, tutoring, delivery, babysitting. They show reliability, working with the public and handling pressure, which every employer values.
- **Volunteering**: community groups, charities, school events, open-source contributions.
- **Clubs and societies**: especially if you held a role, such as treasurer, captain or organiser.
- **Self-taught skills**: online courses, certificates, tools you learned on your own.
- **Languages**: spoken languages with an honest level. The [CEFR guide](/blog/cefr-language-levels-on-your-cv) explains how to describe it.
- **Competitions and awards**: hackathons, olympiads, sports, essay prizes.

None of these needs a job title. They need a clear description of what you did and what came out of it.

## Write the summary last, and keep it plain

A summary is two or three sentences that tell the reader who you are and what you are looking for. Write it after the rest of the document, so you know what you can back up.

```text
Final-year computer science student with experience building a small web app for a student society. Comfortable with JavaScript, Git and basic SQL. Looking for a junior developer internship.
```

```text
Recent business graduate who managed the cash handling and rota for a campus café for two years. Organised, calm under pressure and keen to start in customer support.
```

What to avoid:

- "Hard-working team player seeking a challenging position." It could be about anyone, so it tells the reader nothing.
- Claims you cannot support, such as "expert in marketing" after one course.
- A long objective that repeats your whole document.

The [professional summary guide](/guides/professional-summary-section) goes into more detail.

## Turn activities into bullets that say something

A bullet should show **what you did**, **how much or how well**, and **what changed**. You do not need big numbers. Honest, small ones are fine. If you do not know a number, describe the scope in words instead.

Compare these.

```text
Helped with the society website.
```

```text
Built the society's event page in HTML and CSS and kept it updated, so members could see and sign up for 12 events in the term.
```

```text
Worked at a café.
```

```text
Served about 100 customers on a typical weekend shift and closed the till at the end of the day, with the count matching the records every time.
```

```text
Part of a group project on data.
```

```text
Cleaned a public dataset of 8,000 rows in Python and presented the findings to a class of 40, which earned the highest mark in the group.
```

A few habits help:

- Start with a verb in the past tense: built, organised, wrote, analysed, repaired, taught, handled.
- Name the tool, system or subject, so the reader can picture the work.
- Say how big the thing was: how many people, how long, how many items.
- Say what happened afterwards, if you know. A result is stronger than an activity.
- Keep each bullet to one or two lines.

Only write what is true and what you could explain in an interview. The guide on [work experience](/guides/work-experience-section) covers how to write bullets in more depth, and the same habits apply to projects and volunteering.

## Make education work harder

When you have little else, education carries weight, so give it a few lines of substance.

- Name the degree or programme, the institution and the dates.
- List two to four relevant courses, thesis topics or modules, if they match the job.
- Add a grade only if it helps you. A strong result is worth showing, and a weak one can be left out unless the application asks for it.
- Mention an exchange semester, a scholarship or a leadership role in a student group.

The [education section guide](/guides/education-section) shows how to lay out several entries.

## Use projects as your experience section

For many people, projects are the most convincing part of a first resume. A project shows that you started something, worked through problems and finished.

For each one, give:

- **A name** and one line of context.
- **What you built or did.**
- **The tools or methods** you used.
- **A result or a link**, such as a live page or a code repository, if it exists and you are happy for people to open it.

```text
Budget tracker (personal project)
Built a small web app that imports bank exports and groups spending by category. Used JavaScript and local storage, so no data leaves the browser. Used daily for six months to track my own spending.
```

Only link to work you would be glad for an employer to read. A short, well-explained project is better than a long list of half-finished ones. See the [projects section guide](/guides/projects-section) for more.

## List skills honestly

A skills list should be short and tied to your actual work. Name the tools, languages and methods you have used and could talk about.

- Group them in a few simple lines, such as `Languages`, `Tools` and `Soft skills`, or whatever groups fit your field.
- Avoid skill bars and self-rated percentages. Nobody can compare them, and they invite questions you cannot answer.
- Leave out skills that every candidate lists, such as "Microsoft Office", unless the posting asks for them.
- Make sure each skill in the list also shows up somewhere in your education or projects.

The [skills section guide](/guides/skills-section) explains how to group and order them.

## What to leave out

A first resume is better with fewer lines that all count.

- **Unrelated school achievements** from many years ago, unless they are still relevant.
- **Generic personality words** like "dynamic" or "motivated". Show it in a bullet instead.
- **Long hobby lists.** One or two hobbies that show something relevant are fine. A list of ten is padding.
- **References.** Say "References available on request" only if the application asks. Otherwise the space is better spent on content.
- **Anything untrue.** Invented jobs, inflated titles and borrowed skills are easy to test in an interview and expensive when found out.

Check the conventions for the country and industry you are applying to. In some countries a photo and personal details are normal, and in others they are left off. The [CV vs resume guide](/blog/cv-vs-resume) explains why customs differ.

## A simple layout that works

Choose a layout that is easy to read and easy to import. A single column, a clear heading for each section and a readable font cover most needs. Avoid text inside images, heavy graphics and tables that split your content into hard-to-follow pieces, because those can cause problems when a system reads the file. Nobody can promise how a given employer's software will read your document, so keep the structure plain and the content clear. The [ATS formats guide](/blog/best-cv-formats-for-ats-systems) goes through what tends to go wrong.

If you want a plain design to start from, the `ATS Plain` template in the [template gallery](/cv-templates) keeps the structure simple. You can switch designs at any time without retyping your content.

## Check it before you send it

1. Read it out loud once. Your ear finds clumsy sentences that your eyes skip.
2. Check every date, spelling and capital letter, and make sure your contact details are correct.
3. Ask someone outside your field what job they think you want. If the answer is wrong, change the summary and the order of sections.
4. Open the exported PDF, not the editor, and check page breaks and links.
5. Tailor it. Move the most relevant project or activity to the top for each application, and use words from the posting where they truthfully describe what you did.

The [common mistakes guide](/blog/common-resume-mistakes) has a longer review checklist for the same pass.

## Quick answers

**How long should a first resume be?** One page. If you have a lot of relevant material, two is possible, but it is rarely needed.

**Can I include school projects?** Yes, if they are relevant and you can describe them with specifics. Say what you did, which tools you used and what came out of it.

**Should I put an objective at the top?** A short summary is more useful than a generic objective. Say who you are and what role you are looking for, in two or three sentences.

**What if I really have no activities at all?** Look again. Everyone has done something that took effort: a course, a repair, a trip you planned, a skill you learned from videos. If it is real and you can describe it, it can go on the page. If there is truly nothing, start something small, such as a project or a volunteer shift, and write it up in a few weeks.

**Is it a problem to apply for a role I am not fully qualified for?** Many entry-level postings list more than the employer expects. Apply if you meet most of the core requirements, and be honest about the rest.

## The takeaway

A resume with no job history is a resume built on different evidence. Put your strongest material first, describe it with specifics, keep every claim honest and cut anything that does not help. One clear page is enough.
