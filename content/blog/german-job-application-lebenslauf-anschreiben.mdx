In Germany, Austria and German-speaking Switzerland, an application is a package. It is called a *Bewerbung*, and the core of it is two documents that are meant to be read together: the *Lebenslauf*, which is your CV, and the *Anschreiben*, which is the cover letter. Certificates and references follow behind them.

People who know one half often get the other wrong. They have a strong Lebenslauf and a letter in English layout, or a careful DIN 5008 letter on top of a CV in a foreign format. This guide treats the two as one package. It explains what each part contains, how they should relate, in which order the pages go and what the final file should look like.

## The package at a glance

A complete application for most jobs contains these parts, in this order.

1. **Anschreiben.** One page, addressed to the employer, in the DIN 5008 layout.
2. **Lebenslauf.** One or two pages, in tabular form.
3. **Anlagen.** Attachments such as work references, degree certificates and training certificates.

Some employers add a front page, called a *Deckblatt*, with your name, the position and your photo. It is optional and has become less common. If the posting does not ask for it, you can leave it out and put the photo on the Lebenslauf if you want one.

The order matters because it reflects how the package is read. The letter makes the case, the Lebenslauf supports it with facts and the attachments prove the facts.

## The Lebenslauf

The German CV is the most standardised part of the package. Readers expect to find certain things in certain places.

### Tabular and in reverse order

The Lebenslauf is *tabellarisch*: a table-like layout with dates in a left column and details on the right. Entries run in reverse chronological order, so the latest position comes first. A narrative paragraph form is not used.

### Sections you will usually include

- **Persönliche Daten.** Name, address, phone and email. Date and place of birth and nationality used to be standard. Today they are optional, and many applicants leave them out. Marital status and the number of children are not needed.
- **Berufserfahrung.** Each position with dates in month and year, the employer, your title and two to four lines about what you did and achieved.
- **Ausbildung.** Your degrees and vocational training, with institutions and dates.
- **Kenntnisse.** Skills such as software, tools and certifications. Group them rather than listing everything.
- **Sprachen.** Your languages, with levels from the Common European Framework, such as B2 or C1. The reasoning behind those levels is explained in [CEFR language levels on your CV](/blog/cefr-language-levels-on-your-cv).
- **Weiteres.** Volunteering, publications or interests, only when they relate to the job.

### Photo, signature and place

A photo is still common in Germany, but it is voluntary and nobody can require it. If you use one, choose a plain, professional picture. Whether you add place, date and a signature at the bottom depends on how traditional the employer is. A printed application often carries them. A digital one frequently omits the signature. When in doubt, a typed place and date at the end is harmless.

### Length

One page suits a person at the start of a career. Two pages suit most others. A longer Lebenslauf needs a reason, such as an academic record or a long career that cannot be shortened without losing relevant facts.

## The Anschreiben

The cover letter has its own rules, which fit in a separate article. In short: one page, a subject line instead of a heading, the recipient's address in a fixed zone and a formal salutation and closing. The details are in [the German cover letter and DIN 5008](/blog/german-cover-letter-din-5008), and the writing advice that applies in every language is in [how to write a cover letter that gets read](/blog/how-to-write-a-cover-letter).

What matters here is how the letter works with the Lebenslauf.

- **It does not repeat it.** The Lebenslauf lists the facts. The letter picks two or three and explains why they matter for this job.
- **It points to it.** A sentence such as "Details zu meinem Werdegang finden Sie im beigefügten Lebenslauf" is enough for the reader to know where to look. Many writers use the closing for this and for the attachments list.
- **It uses the same facts.** Titles, dates and employer names must match.

## Make the two documents match

A recruiter often has both documents open next to each other. Visible differences make an application look assembled, and differences in facts raise questions that you do not want to answer in an interview.

Check these points before sending.

- **Name and contact details.** The same spelling, the same phone number and the same email in both. A letter with an old number is a real way to miss a call.
- **Typography.** The same font family and similar sizes. A letter in one typeface and a Lebenslauf in another look like they were written by two people.
- **Colour.** If your Lebenslauf has an accent colour, use it for the letter's header, too, and nowhere else.
- **Dates and titles.** The role you name in the letter is the role that appears in the Lebenslauf, with the same dates.
- **Language.** Both documents in German, or both in English. A German letter on an English CV is only acceptable when the posting itself is bilingual.
- **Photo.** If both carry one, it is the same photo. Most people put it on the Lebenslauf only.

Keeping these in sync by hand is error-prone, which is why it helps to let one document supply the details for the other. ## Attachments

Under *Anlagen* you put documents that prove what you claim. What is expected depends on the field and the level.

- **Arbeitszeugnisse.** Work references from employers, which are a standard part of the German system. Include them for the most recent and most relevant positions.
- **Zeugnisse and certificates from education.** Degree certificates and transcripts when they are asked for or relevant.
- **Weiterbildungsnachweise.** Certificates of courses and certifications that support the job.

Include only what supports this application. A thick stack of unrelated papers does not help. If a document is not in German or English, check whether the employer wants a translation.

## One file, not five

Most employers now accept online applications, and many ask for a single PDF. If the posting does not say, a single PDF is the safest choice, because it keeps the order you chose and cannot be opened in the wrong sequence.

- **Order the pages as above:** letter, Lebenslauf, attachments.
- **Keep the size reasonable.** A few megabytes is typical, and some forms limit it. Compress scanned certificates rather than sending photographs of them.
- **Name the file clearly.** Your name and the word Bewerbung, for example `Bewerbung_Muster_Max.pdf`, is easy to find in a crowded inbox.
- **Check the PDF on screen.** Open it, look at every page and make sure text is selectable. Some systems read text from the PDF, and an image of text cannot be read.
- **Use A4.** The standard paper size in the region, and the size the DIN 5008 layout is defined for.

If you apply by email, put a short note in the body that names the position and points to the attachment. Do not paste the whole letter into the email and attach it again.

## A checklist before you send

- The posting's title, reference number and contact person are copied correctly.
- The Anschreiben has the subject line, the right salutation and the right closing.
- The company name is right everywhere it appears.
- The Lebenslauf is tabular, in reverse order and no longer than two pages.
- Names, contact details, dates and titles match in both documents.
- Both documents share one typeface and one accent colour.
- The language levels use CEFR codes.
- The attachments support this job and are in the order you listed.
- The final PDF opens, reads correctly and has a clear name.

## Applying in more than one country

If you apply both in Germany and elsewhere, do not try to make one package serve both. A German Lebenslauf and an English CV differ in length, order of detail and in how personal information is treated. Keep one CV for each market, with a letter for every application under the CV it belongs to. The reasoning for that structure is in [one CV, many cover letters](/blog/one-cv-many-cover-letters).
