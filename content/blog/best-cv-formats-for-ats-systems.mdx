When people talk about ATS-friendly CV formats, they often assume the answer is a special template. In practice, the best format is usually the one that preserves content hierarchy and keeps the parser from guessing.

That means your format should make the document easy for both machines and humans to read. Recruiters still make the final decision, so a CV that survives parsing but feels confusing on screen is not enough.

## What an ATS actually does

An applicant tracking system, or ATS, is software that collects applications, reads each CV into structured fields such as name, employers, dates and skills, and lets recruiters search, sort and track candidates. For many employers it is closer to a database with a search box than to a robot that decides who is good.

How a system treats your CV depends on the product, on how the employer set it up and on who is using it. Some rank or filter candidates using keywords or screening questions. Many mainly store and display applications for a person to read. Because you cannot tell which kind you are facing, the sensible goal is a CV that any parser reads correctly and that a person can scan quickly.

## The safest default format

For most candidates, the safest format includes:

1. Contact details at the top
2. A short summary or headline
3. Experience in reverse chronological order
4. Education
5. Skills
6. Projects or certifications when relevant

This order works because it mirrors how most hiring teams review candidates. It also maps cleanly to the fields many ATS products expect: a parser that finds a heading called `Experience` followed by employers, titles and dates has little to guess about.

## What to avoid

The most common formatting issues are not dramatic. They are small layout choices that create friction.

- Multi-column layouts for critical text
- Icons without text labels
- Headers embedded in images
- Decorative tables for core sections
- Unclear date formats

If a section matters, do not hide it behind a layout trick.

Each of these has a plain reason behind it. Software reads a page as a stream of text, so when two columns sit side by side, a parser may read across the page and mix a job title with a skill list. An icon next to an email address means nothing to a parser unless the address itself is written out. Text inside an image is not text at all. And a date written as `3/4/22` can mean two different months depending on the country, so write `03/2022` or `March 2022`.

Modern systems cope with more layouts than older ones did, and some handle simple columns well. That is not a reason to rely on it. A single column costs you very little and removes the risk.

## Format choices that usually work well

### Reverse chronological

This is still the best default for most professionals because it makes progression obvious. Recruiters understand it immediately and ATS tools usually map it well. Put the most detail into the last ten or so years, and keep older roles short.

### Hybrid or combination

This can work for career changers or candidates with strong projects, but only if the experience section still remains concrete and easy to follow. A common version puts a short `Selected projects` or `Key skills in action` section above the work history, so the reader sees relevant proof first and still finds the dates they expect below.

### Functional

This format is risky for most job seekers. It can hide timelines, which makes recruiters suspicious even when the experience is legitimate. If you have gaps or are changing field, the hybrid format gives you the same benefit without the doubt.

## File format: PDF or DOCX?

Most employers accept both, and some state a preference in the advert, so follow that first. A PDF looks the same on every device. A DOCX can be edited by a recruiter and is sometimes requested explicitly.

Whichever you send, the text inside it must be real text, not a picture of text. A PDF produced by scanning a printout, or exported as an image, cannot be read by a parser. The quickest check is a copy-and-paste test: open the file, select all, copy, and paste into a plain text editor. If the words appear in a sensible order, a parser can probably read them too. If they come out scrambled, missing or not at all, change the template or the way you export.

## Test your own CV in five minutes

1. **Copy and paste.** Paste the whole document into a plain text editor and read it from top to bottom. Is the order right?
2. **Check the fields.** Can you tell, from the pasted text alone, where each job starts, what it was called and when it began and ended?
3. **Check the links.** Click every link in the file you will send.
4. **Check the name.** A file called `Jana-Novak-CV.pdf` is easier for a recruiter to find than `resume_final2.pdf`.
5. **Ask a person.** Hand it to someone and ask them to tell you, in ten seconds, what job you want.

## Headings and wording

Use standard section names such as `Experience`, `Education` and `Skills`. A heading like `My Journey` is more charming and harder to map. Do the same with job titles. If your employer used an unusual internal title, you can add the common equivalent in brackets, as long as it is true, for example `Customer Happiness Lead (Customer Support Team Lead)`.

Keywords matter less than people think, and more than none. Read the advert, and make sure the terms that are true for you appear in the place where you used them. The mistake of packing in terms without evidence is covered in our guide to [common resume mistakes](/blog/common-resume-mistakes).

## Myths worth ignoring

**"The system rejects most applications automatically."** You will often see an exact percentage attached to this claim. In most cases a person decides, and the system mainly makes candidates searchable. Treat precise statistics with suspicion, and aim for a CV that is easy to read for both.

**"You need a special ATS template."** A clear single-column document with standard headings is already most of the way there. Paying for a template that promises to beat the system is rarely worth it.

**"Hide keywords in white text."** Some advice suggests copying the advert into the page in white text. Anyone who opens the plain text version sees it, and it tends to read as an attempt to game the process. If a keyword is true for you, put it in a bullet where it belongs.

**"You can skip the application form."** Many systems ask you to upload a CV and then retype the details into a form. The form is often what the system stores most reliably, so fill it in fully and consistently with your CV, and do not write "see CV".

## Templates are less important than discipline

A good template helps, but it does not save weak structure. Keep the choice practical and avoid anything that treats visual complexity as a differentiator. A plain single-column template, such as ATS Plain in EngineCV, does the job. What matters more than the template is that your headings are standard, your dates are consistent and your bullets are specific.
